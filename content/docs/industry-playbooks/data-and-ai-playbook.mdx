# Data & AI Playbook

> This is a sales playbook, not technical training — see [Industry Discovery Framework](/docs/industry-playbooks/industry-discovery-framework) for the standard this article follows. Nothing here states specific tools, methods, or market claims as fact; questions requiring real technical judgment go to an internal technical SME.

## Typical Candidate Profile

This playbook covers a wide range of candidates working in data — from analysts and data engineers to data scientists and candidates working specifically in AI or machine learning. The label "data and AI" spans very different day-to-day work, and discovery should clarify exactly where a given candidate fits rather than assuming.

## Career Mindset

Many candidates in this space, particularly those closer to AI and machine learning, are navigating a field that's received an unusual amount of public attention recently, which can create both opportunity and noise. Some candidates feel genuine excitement about being in a fast-moving field; others feel real pressure to keep up with a pace of change that can feel relentless.

## Buying Behaviour

Candidates in this field often want to understand specifically how a program or opportunity connects to the type of data work they actually do, since "data" can mean very different things across roles. Given the field's high public visibility, candidates are also often more skeptical of hype-driven claims, and respond better to grounded, specific conversations than enthusiastic generalities.

## Biggest Career Pain Points

- Ambiguity around what their specific role actually covers, especially when job titles in this space vary so much between companies
- Concern about how fast the field is moving and whether their current skill set is keeping pace
- Difficulty finding a program or advisor who understands the difference between adjacent specializations within data and AI, rather than treating them as one category
- Skepticism toward inflated claims about the field, given how much hype surrounds it publicly

## Discovery Questions

1. "How would you describe your specific role within data or AI — what does your day-to-day actually involve?"
2. "What draws you to this field specifically, versus a more general software or analytics path?"
3. "How do you feel about how quickly things are changing in this space — is that exciting, stressful, or some of both?"
4. "What's been the biggest challenge in your job search so far, if you've started one?"
5. "Is there anything about your technical background you'd want a specialist's opinion on, rather than mine?"

## Qualification Signals

- The candidate can describe their actual work with real specificity, distinguishing their role from adjacent ones
- The candidate demonstrates grounded, rather than hype-driven, thinking about the field
- The candidate's account of their experience stays consistent as the conversation goes deeper

## Risk Signals

- The candidate's description of their technical work is vague or seems to borrow heavily from buzzwords rather than lived experience
- The candidate is targeting a specialization that seems disconnected from their described background, without explanation
- The candidate asks a specific technical question the advisor isn't equipped to answer accurately

See [Industry Red Flags](/docs/industry-playbooks/industry-red-flags) for what to do when a technical claim needs real verification.

## Presentation Strategy

Avoid hype-driven language entirely — candidates in this field are often more skeptical of exaggerated claims than most, given how much noise surrounds their industry publicly. Ground the recommendation in the candidate's actual described work and goals, and be direct that deeper technical evaluation belongs to an internal SME.

## Common Objections

Given the field's visibility and the number of programs claiming expertise in it, [Trust and Credibility](/docs/objections/trust-and-credibility) concerns come up often. Candidates may also raise [No Guarantee Concern](/docs/objections/no-guarantee-concern) given how frequently outsized promises circulate in this space.

## Closing Strategy

Confirm the recommendation reflects the candidate's actual specialization within data and AI, not a generic version of the field. If a technical claim central to the recommendation hasn't been verified by an SME, resolve that before closing.

## Advisor Mistakes

- Treating "data" or "AI" as a single category instead of asking what the candidate's specific role actually involves
- Using hype-driven language that this population is often quick to distrust
- Guessing at how a candidate's specific technical background compares to current market demand
- Failing to distinguish between adjacent specializations within the field

## Key Takeaways

Data and AI candidates span a wide range of specific roles, and treating the field as one category is a fast way to lose credibility. Ground conversations in the candidate's actual work, avoid hype-driven language, and route any real technical evaluation to an internal SME rather than guessing.
