# Software Engineering Playbook

> This is a sales playbook, not technical training — see [Industry Discovery Framework](/docs/industry-playbooks/industry-discovery-framework) for the standard this article follows. Nothing here describes specific technologies, salary levels, or hiring requirements as fact; any question that needs real technical judgment goes to an internal technical SME.

## Typical Candidate Profile

Candidates in this playbook range from early-career developers to experienced engineers, spanning many different specializations, languages, and levels of seniority. "Software engineer" is a broad label, and this playbook is meant to guide the conversation that clarifies what it actually means for the candidate in front of you, not to assume a single profile.

## Career Mindset

Many candidates in this field take real pride in their craft and want to be evaluated on the substance of their work, not just a title or years of experience. There's often an underlying awareness that the field moves quickly, which can create a quiet anxiety about staying current, even among candidates with strong, well-established skills.

## Buying Behaviour

Candidates in this field tend to be comparison-driven and detail-oriented — they're often evaluating multiple options at once and paying close attention to specifics, so vague claims tend to lose credibility fast. A conversation that demonstrates real curiosity about their actual work tends to build trust faster than one that leans on generic industry language.

## Biggest Career Pain Points

- Feeling like their specific experience gets flattened into a generic "developer" category by recruiters and platforms alike
- Uncertainty about how their particular background stacks up in a market that can feel like it's always shifting
- Application fatigue from high-volume, low-response job searching, especially in more competitive specializations
- A sense that some employers screen on narrow keyword matches rather than actual capability

## Discovery Questions

1. "What would you say your specialization is, and how did you end up there?"
2. "Walk me through your current role — what do you actually spend most of your time doing?"
3. "What's been the hardest part of your job search so far, if you've already started?"
4. "Is there a type of team or company culture you're specifically looking for, beyond the role itself?"
5. "Is there anything technical about your background or target roles you'd want a specialist's opinion on, rather than mine?"

## Qualification Signals

- The candidate can describe their actual day-to-day work with specificity, not just list technologies
- The candidate's account of their experience stays consistent and detailed as the conversation goes deeper
- The candidate has a grounded sense of their own level, even if they're still refining their target

## Risk Signals

- The candidate's description of their technical background feels vague, inconsistent, or rehearsed rather than lived-in
- The candidate is targeting a level or specialization that seems significantly disconnected from their described experience
- The candidate asks a specific technical question the advisor can't answer with confidence

See [Industry Red Flags](/docs/industry-playbooks/industry-red-flags) for what to do when a technical claim needs real verification.

## Presentation Strategy

Lead with genuine curiosity about the candidate's actual work rather than trying to demonstrate technical fluency the advisor doesn't have. Connect the recommendation to what the candidate has described about their own experience and goals, and be explicit that any deeper technical assessment belongs to an internal SME, not the advisor's own judgment.

## Common Objections

Candidates in this field often have a strong independent job search underway already — see [Already Applying Myself](/docs/objections/already-applying-myself). Given how comparison-driven this population tends to be, cost and value objections are also common — see [Price Objection](/docs/objections/price-objection).

## Closing Strategy

Confirm the recommendation is grounded in what the candidate has actually described about their background and goals, not in an unverified technical assessment. If a technical question central to the decision came up during the call, make sure it's been routed to an SME before treating the conversation as fully resolved.

## Advisor Mistakes

- Trying to sound like a technical expert instead of asking genuine, curious questions
- Treating all software engineering candidates as interchangeable, regardless of specialization or level
- Guessing at how a candidate's specific technical background compares to market demand
- Dismissing a candidate's detailed account of their work in favor of a generic pitch

## Key Takeaways

Software engineering candidates are detail-oriented and comparison-driven, and they respond to genuine curiosity about their actual work far better than to generic industry language. Never assess technical fit or claims on the advisor's own authority — route anything requiring real technical judgment to an internal SME.
