# Business Analyst Playbook

> This is a sales playbook, not technical training — see [Industry Discovery Framework](/docs/industry-playbooks/industry-discovery-framework) for the standard this article follows. Nothing here states specific methodologies, tools, or requirements as fact; questions requiring real domain expertise go to an internal SME.

## Typical Candidate Profile

Candidates in this playbook work at the intersection of business needs and technical or operational systems, translating between the two. The role varies significantly across industries and companies — some business analyst roles lean heavily technical, others lean heavily toward stakeholder management and process design — and this playbook is meant to help clarify which applies to a given candidate.

## Career Mindset

Many candidates in this field think of themselves as translators and problem-solvers, comfortable moving between technical and non-technical conversations, and they often evaluate a career opportunity the same way — by trying to understand the real situation before forming a view. This can make them thoughtful, thorough evaluators who appreciate a conversation that mirrors that same care.

## Buying Behaviour

Candidates in this space often respond well to a structured, well-organized conversation, since that mirrors the kind of work they do themselves. They tend to notice quickly if a conversation feels disorganized or generic, since spotting exactly that kind of gap between stated goals and actual execution is core to their professional skill set.

## Biggest Career Pain Points

- Difficulty communicating the specific value of translation and stakeholder-facing work to employers focused primarily on technical output
- Uncertainty about how their particular mix of technical and business skills compares to what a target role actually wants
- A sense that the role can be interpreted so differently across companies that it's hard to know what "business analyst" will mean in a new context
- Frustration with vague or disorganized outreach from recruiters, given how much this population values clarity

## Discovery Questions

1. "How would you describe your specific mix of technical and business-facing work?"
2. "What kinds of stakeholders do you typically work with, and what does that collaboration actually look like?"
3. "Have you found that 'business analyst' means different things at different companies you've worked at or considered?"
4. "What's the clearest example of a problem you've solved by translating between business and technical sides?"
5. "Is there anything about your background you'd want a specialist's opinion on, rather than mine?"

## Qualification Signals

- The candidate can describe specific examples of translating between business and technical needs
- The candidate is thoughtful and precise in how they describe their own skill mix
- The candidate evaluates the conversation itself with the same structured thinking they apply professionally

## Risk Signals

- The candidate's description of their role is vague or seems to rely on the job title alone rather than specific examples
- The candidate is targeting a role that assumes a level of technical depth not reflected in their described background, or the reverse
- The candidate asks a specific technical or methodological question the advisor isn't equipped to answer accurately

See [Industry Red Flags](/docs/industry-playbooks/industry-red-flags) for what to do when a claim needs real verification.

## Presentation Strategy

Be structured and specific — this population tends to notice, and respond well to, a conversation that's organized the way they'd organize their own work. Clarify early where a candidate actually sits on the technical-to-stakeholder-facing spectrum, since "business analyst" can mean meaningfully different things.

## Common Objections

Candidates in this field are often careful, deliberate decision-makers, so [Need Time to Think](/docs/objections/need-time-to-think) is common and usually genuine rather than a soft decline. Given how much this role varies by company, [Trust and Credibility](/docs/objections/trust-and-credibility) can also surface if a candidate isn't confident an advisor understands the role's real variation.

## Closing Strategy

Confirm the recommendation reflects where the candidate actually sits on the technical-to-stakeholder-facing spectrum, not an assumed version of the role. Route any unresolved technical or methodological claim to an SME before closing.

## Advisor Mistakes

- Treating "business analyst" as a single, well-defined role instead of asking how it plays out for this specific candidate
- Delivering a disorganized or generic pitch to a population that notices and values structure
- Guessing at how a candidate's specific skill mix compares to a target role's actual requirements
- Missing specific examples in favor of accepting a job title at face value

## Key Takeaways

Business analyst candidates work at the intersection of business and technical needs, and the role varies enough by company that assuming a single definition is a mistake. Be structured and specific, clarify where a candidate sits on the technical-to-stakeholder-facing spectrum, and route real technical or methodological questions to an internal SME.
