# Project Manager Playbook

> This is a sales playbook, not technical or methodology training — see [Industry Discovery Framework](/docs/industry-playbooks/industry-discovery-framework) for the standard this article follows. Nothing here states specific methodologies, certifications, or requirements as fact; questions requiring real domain expertise go to an internal SME.

## Typical Candidate Profile

Candidates in this playbook manage delivery of projects or programs, often across technical teams, and range from candidates managing a single workstream to those overseeing large, cross-functional programs. Industry and methodology background vary significantly, and this playbook is meant to guide a conversation that clarifies specifics rather than assuming a single project management style.

## Career Mindset

Many candidates in this field are naturally process-oriented and tend to evaluate a career decision the way they'd evaluate a project — looking for a clear plan, a realistic timeline, and honest visibility into risk. A vague or overly optimistic pitch is likely to read, to this population, the same way an unrealistic project plan would.

## Buying Behaviour

Candidates in this space often want to see the "plan" behind a recommendation — what happens, in what order, and what the real risks and dependencies are — mirroring how they'd expect a project to be presented to them. They tend to respond poorly to pressure or artificial urgency, since managing exactly that kind of pressure without giving in to it is often part of their own professional skill set.

## Biggest Career Pain Points

- Difficulty differentiating themselves when project management skills can sound generic across very different industries and company types
- Uncertainty about how a specific methodology background (or lack of a specific one) affects their competitiveness for a given role
- A sense that project management is sometimes seen as interchangeable across industries, when the actual context and stakes vary enormously
- Frustration with vague timelines or plans from recruiters or programs, given how central clear planning is to their own professional standards

## Discovery Questions

1. "What industries or types of projects have you managed, and how has that shaped your approach?"
2. "How would you describe your methodology background, if you have a specific one?"
3. "What does a realistic, honest project plan look like to you — and does that match how you'd want this conversation to go?"
4. "What's the biggest risk or dependency in your current job search, the way you'd frame it if this were a project?"
5. "Is there anything about your background you'd want a specialist's opinion on, rather than mine?"

## Qualification Signals

- The candidate can describe specific projects, scope, and outcomes rather than only general responsibilities
- The candidate applies the same structured thinking to evaluating this conversation that they'd apply to their own work
- The candidate's account of their methodology background and experience is specific and consistent

## Risk Signals

- The candidate's description of their project scope or outcomes is vague or difficult to pin down
- The candidate is targeting a role or industry with significantly different stakes or complexity than their described experience, without explanation
- The candidate asks a specific methodology or industry question the advisor isn't equipped to answer accurately

See [Industry Red Flags](/docs/industry-playbooks/industry-red-flags) for what to do when a claim needs real verification.

## Presentation Strategy

Present the recommendation the way this population would want a project presented to them — a clear plan, honest visibility into risk, and no artificial urgency. Avoid vague timelines or plans, since this is a population unusually well-equipped to notice exactly that kind of gap.

## Common Objections

Given this population's discomfort with pressure, [No Guarantee Concern](/docs/objections/no-guarantee-concern) often comes up framed in risk-management terms rather than as pure skepticism. See [Closing Call Checklist](/docs/closing/closing-call-checklist) for making sure next steps are communicated with the same clarity this population expects from a project plan.

## Closing Strategy

Present next steps as clearly and specifically as this population would expect from any project plan — vague next steps are likely to undermine confidence built earlier in the call. Route any unresolved technical or methodology claim to an SME before closing.

## Advisor Mistakes

- Presenting a vague plan or timeline to a population that evaluates exactly that kind of clarity professionally
- Using pressure or artificial urgency with a population skilled at recognizing and resisting it
- Treating project management as interchangeable across industries and stakes levels
- Guessing at how a candidate's specific methodology background compares to a target role's actual requirements

## Key Takeaways

Project manager candidates evaluate a career decision the way they'd evaluate a project — looking for a clear plan, honest risk visibility, and no artificial urgency. Present the recommendation and next steps with that same clarity, and route methodology or industry-specific questions to an internal SME rather than guessing.
