# Cybersecurity Playbook

> This is a sales playbook, not technical training — see [Industry Discovery Framework](/docs/industry-playbooks/industry-discovery-framework) for the standard this article follows. Nothing here states specific certifications, tools, or requirements as fact; questions requiring real technical judgment go to an internal technical SME.

## Typical Candidate Profile

Candidates in this field range across many specializations — from security analysts and engineers to candidates focused on governance, risk, and compliance, or on specific areas like penetration testing. Cybersecurity is a broad field with real internal variation, and discovery should establish exactly where a candidate fits rather than assuming.

## Career Mindset

Many candidates in this field think in terms of risk and precision by professional habit, and that mindset often carries directly into how they evaluate a career opportunity or a program — carefully, and with real scrutiny of specific claims. There's often also a strong sense of professional identity tied to trust and integrity, given the nature of the work.

## Buying Behaviour

Candidates in this field tend to be thorough evaluators who dig into specifics before committing to anything, and they're often unusually good at spotting vague or overconfident claims, given that's part of their professional training. A conversation that's honest about its own limits tends to land better here than one that tries to sound more authoritative than it actually is.

## Biggest Career Pain Points

- Difficulty communicating the value of highly specialized work to people outside the field, including some recruiters and hiring processes
- A sense that the field is sometimes reduced to a narrow set of certifications or buzzwords rather than actual demonstrated capability
- Concern about how their specific specialization within cybersecurity maps to real market demand
- Frustration with generic outreach that clearly wasn't tailored to their actual specialization

## Discovery Questions

1. "What's your specific specialization within cybersecurity, and how did you land there?"
2. "Walk me through what a typical week actually looks like in your current role."
3. "What's been the most frustrating part of how recruiters or programs have approached you in this field?"
4. "Is there a specific type of organization or environment you're targeting — for example, based on industry or company size?"
5. "Is there anything technical about your background you'd want verified by a specialist rather than taking my word for it?"

## Qualification Signals

- The candidate can describe their specialization and daily work with real precision
- The candidate asks sharp, specific questions that suggest genuine evaluation rather than passive interest
- The candidate's account of their experience holds up consistently as the conversation goes deeper

## Risk Signals

- The candidate's description of their technical background feels generic or doesn't hold up under a few follow-up questions
- The candidate is targeting a specialization that seems disconnected from their described experience
- The candidate asks a specific technical or compliance-related question the advisor isn't equipped to answer accurately

See [Industry Red Flags](/docs/industry-playbooks/industry-red-flags) for what to do when a technical claim needs real verification.

## Presentation Strategy

Be direct about what the advisor can and can't speak to with authority — this population tends to respect that kind of honesty more than a confident-sounding but shallow attempt at technical credibility. Ground the recommendation in the candidate's specific specialization rather than a generic "cybersecurity" pitch.

## Common Objections

Given this population's tendency toward careful scrutiny, [No Guarantee Concern](/docs/objections/no-guarantee-concern) and [Trust and Credibility](/docs/objections/trust-and-credibility) both come up often and deserve direct, specific answers rather than reassurance.

## Closing Strategy

Confirm the recommendation is grounded in the candidate's specific specialization and actual described experience, not a generalized version of the field. Route any unresolved technical claim to an SME before treating the conversation as settled.

## Advisor Mistakes

- Treating cybersecurity as one undifferentiated field rather than asking about specific specialization
- Trying to sound more technically authoritative than the advisor actually is, with a population well-equipped to notice
- Using generic outreach or framing that doesn't reflect the candidate's actual specific work
- Guessing at how a candidate's specific background compares to market demand

Cybersecurity candidates evaluate carefully and are often skilled at spotting vague or overconfident claims — meet that with honesty about what the advisor can and can't speak to, and ground every conversation in the candidate's specific specialization. Route technical and compliance-related questions to an internal SME rather than guessing.
