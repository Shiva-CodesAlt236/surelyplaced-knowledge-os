# Industry Discovery Framework

> **This module is a sales playbook, not technical training.** Every article in the Industry Playbooks module teaches an Admissions Advisor how candidates from a given field tend to think, evaluate options, and make decisions — not the technical substance of that field. Advisors are never expected to verify a candidate's technical claims, assess their skill level, or speak with authority about tools, certifications, or role requirements. Any question that needs real technical judgment goes to an internal technical subject matter expert, not an advisor's best guess.

## Typical Candidate Profile

This article doesn't describe one candidate profile — it describes the discovery approach that applies before a specific industry background is even identified. Every candidate has a stated field, role history, and target, and none of it should be assumed from a job title alone.

## Career Mindset

Across nearly every field, candidates evaluate a career decision partly on the substance of the opportunity and partly on whether the person guiding them actually understands their world. An advisor doesn't need deep technical fluency to earn that trust — genuine curiosity about the candidate's actual day-to-day work usually does more than a surface-level attempt to sound like an expert.

## Buying Behaviour

Buying behavior varies by field in ways that are worth understanding at a pattern level — some fields reward candidates who move fast on a strong opportunity, others reward candidates who are more deliberate and comparison-driven. What stays constant is that candidates buy based on whether the conversation reflects their actual situation, not a generic version of "tech candidates" or "professionals."

## Biggest Career Pain Points

- Feeling reduced to a job title or keyword match rather than understood as a specific person with specific experience
- Uncertainty about how their particular background compares to what the current market actually wants
- Difficulty finding anyone — recruiter, advisor, or otherwise — who asks real questions before making a recommendation
- Skepticism built from prior interactions with recruiters who oversold their understanding of a technical field they didn't actually know well

## Discovery Questions

1. "What field or specialization would you say you're in, in your own words?"
2. "Walk me through what you actually do day to day in your current or most recent role."
3. "What's changed in your field recently that's affected how you think about your own career?"
4. "How do you think your background compares to what employers in this space are currently looking for?"
5. "Is there anything technical about your target roles that you're not sure I'd fully understand — and if so, is that something we should loop in someone else on?"

## Qualification Signals

- The candidate can describe their work with real specificity, not just a title or buzzwords
- The candidate's stated experience is internally consistent as the conversation goes deeper
- The candidate is asking questions that suggest they want an informed conversation, not just validation

## Risk Signals

- The candidate's description of their technical background doesn't add up, or shifts noticeably as they're asked more detail
- The candidate is targeting a role that seems significantly disconnected from their described experience, without an explanation
- The candidate asks the advisor a specific technical question that requires real expertise to answer accurately

See [Industry Red Flags](/docs/industry-playbooks/industry-red-flags) for the full list of signals that mean a claim should be routed to an internal technical SME rather than accepted or guessed at.

## Presentation Strategy

Never present a recommendation that depends on the advisor's own technical judgment about a candidate's skill level or fit for a specific technical role. Build the recommendation around what the candidate has told you and what Surely Placed provides structurally — readiness support, positioning, access — not around an advisor's personal assessment of their technical strength.

## Common Objections

Objections rooted in a genuine mismatch between a candidate's background and their target are not something to argue past — they're useful, honest signals. See the field-specific playbooks in this module for the objection patterns typical of each one.

## Closing Strategy

No closing conversation should proceed around an unverified technical claim central to the recommendation. If a claim needs real verification, route it to an internal technical SME before treating it as settled.

## Advisor Mistakes

- Trying to sound technically fluent in a field the advisor doesn't actually understand well, instead of asking genuine questions
- Treating every candidate in a broad field (like "software" or "healthcare") as the same, without asking about their actual specialization
- Guessing at a technical answer to avoid an awkward "I'm not sure, let me find out" moment
- Building a recommendation on a technical claim that was never actually verified

## Key Takeaways

Every field-specific playbook in this module operates under the same standard: understand how candidates in that field think and decide, without ever claiming technical authority the advisor doesn't have. Ask genuine questions, take the candidate's own account seriously, and route anything requiring real technical judgment to an internal SME rather than guessing.
