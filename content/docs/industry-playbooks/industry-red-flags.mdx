# Industry Red Flags

> This article is compliance-adjacent, not technical training. It teaches advisors when to stop relying on their own judgment about a technical or industry-specific claim and route it to an internal subject matter expert instead. It does not teach advisors how to evaluate the claim themselves.

## Typical Candidate Profile

This article applies to any candidate, in any field covered by this module, whose discovery conversation surfaces a claim or question that goes beyond what an advisor can responsibly assess on their own.

## Career Mindset

A candidate whose claim triggers one of these signals isn't necessarily doing anything wrong — sometimes a background is genuinely unusual, a transition is genuinely non-linear, or a technical detail is genuinely outside common knowledge. The mindset to bring to this article is curiosity and care, not suspicion.

## Buying Behaviour

Not the relevant frame here — this article exists to protect the integrity of buying decisions made elsewhere in the funnel by making sure no recommendation gets built on an unverified technical or industry claim.

## Biggest Career Pain Points

- Candidates whose real, valid background doesn't fit a common pattern may worry that an advisor will misjudge them as a result
- Candidates may not realize a specific claim they've made requires verification, and may interpret a pause as distrust rather than diligence
- Advisors under call-pressure may feel tempted to accept an unclear claim rather than pause to verify it

## Discovery Questions

1. "Can you walk me through that in a bit more detail, so I understand it accurately?"
2. "Is this something you'd want a specialist to weigh in on, or does my understanding sound right to you?"
3. "Has anyone else you've talked to raised questions about this part of your background?"
4. "Is there documentation or a portfolio that reflects this, that could help make it concrete?"

## Qualification Signals

Signals that a claim is likely accurate and doesn't need escalation:

- The candidate can provide specific, consistent detail when asked to elaborate
- The candidate has documentation, a portfolio, or a reference point they can point to
- The claim, while perhaps unusual, has an internally coherent explanation

## Risk Signals

The following mean a claim should be routed to an internal technical SME rather than accepted or guessed at:

- A specific technical claim central to the candidate's target role that the advisor can't personally verify
- A claim that seems inconsistent with the rest of the candidate's described background, without a clear explanation
- A candidate directly asking the advisor to confirm or validate a technical or industry-specific detail
- A claim involving licensure, certification, or regulatory status in a field where that matters (see [Healthcare Playbook](/docs/industry-playbooks/healthcare-playbook) for one example)
- Any moment where the advisor notices they're about to guess rather than answer from real knowledge

## Presentation Strategy

When a red flag surfaces, say plainly that the specific detail needs to be confirmed by someone with real expertise, rather than smoothing over the moment or guessing to keep the conversation moving. Offer to follow up with an accurate answer rather than leaving the candidate with an unverified one.

## Common Objections

Some candidates may feel that a pause to verify something reads as distrust — address this directly and warmly rather than assuming it will land as an accusation. See [Trust and Credibility](/docs/objections/trust-and-credibility) for the broader pattern of handling skepticism about accuracy calmly and specifically.

## Closing Strategy

Never close a conversation around a recommendation that depends on an unverified technical or industry-specific claim. If one is outstanding, resolve it with an SME before treating the recommendation as settled.

## Advisor Mistakes

- Guessing at a technical answer to avoid an awkward pause in the conversation
- Treating an unusual but valid background as suspicious rather than simply asking more questions
- Failing to follow up with an accurate answer after telling a candidate something would be confirmed
- Assuming a claim is fine because it sounded confident, rather than because it held up to specific questions

## Key Takeaways

When a technical or industry-specific claim goes beyond what an advisor can responsibly verify, the response is the same every time: say so plainly, route it to an internal SME, and follow up with an accurate answer rather than guessing to keep the conversation moving. An unusual background isn't automatically a red flag — a claim that can't be verified by the person relying on it is.
