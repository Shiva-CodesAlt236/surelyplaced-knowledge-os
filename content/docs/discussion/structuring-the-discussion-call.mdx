# Structuring the Discussion Call

## Purpose

Discovery gathers the picture. The discussion call is where that picture gets handed back to the candidate in the form of an actual recommendation — what Surely Placed is, how the process works, and what it would take from them. This article is about the architecture of that call: what order things need to happen in, and why getting the order wrong quietly undermines a conversation that might otherwise go well.

This isn't a script. It's a sequence, and the sequence matters because each part of a discussion call depends on the one before it landing first. You can't discuss investment honestly before the candidate understands the process. You can't explain the process convincingly before you've shown them you actually understood their discovery conversation. Structure isn't a formality here — it's what makes the recommendation believable.

## Learning Objectives

- Lay out the four-part shape of a well-run discussion call and explain why that order holds
- Open a discussion call by demonstrating what was retained from discovery, not by starting fresh
- Recognize when a call is drifting out of sequence and bring it back without making the shift feel abrupt
- Know when a discussion call should pause and route back to discovery instead of continuing forward
- Hand a call off cleanly to a closing conversation once discussion is genuinely complete

## Candidate Psychology

A candidate walking into a discussion call is testing something specific: did the last conversation actually go anywhere? Discovery calls ask a lot of a candidate — background, goals, sometimes uncomfortable details about authorization status or employment history. If the discussion call opens without any visible sign that information was used, the candidate reasonably concludes the discovery call was a formality, not a real diagnostic step. That reaction is fast and it's hard to undo later in the same call.

There's also a pacing expectation candidates bring into this call that's worth naming directly. By the time someone agrees to a second, longer conversation, they've usually decided they want a real recommendation, not another round of general information. A discussion call that spends too long re-explaining basics, or that jumps around between topics without a clear throughline, reads as either disorganized or evasive — neither of which the candidate is likely to say out loud, but both of which show up as quiet disengagement.

Finally, candidates are listening for the order things come up in as a signal in itself. A call that jumps to cost early, before the candidate understands what they'd actually be paying for, tends to put candidates on the defensive regardless of how reasonable the number eventually turns out to be. Sequence isn't just organizational — it shapes how everything that follows gets received.

## Advisor Objective

Run the discussion call in a sequence that lets each part build credibility for the next — opening with what was learned in discovery, moving to a tailored recommendation, then process, then investment — so that by the time cost comes up, the candidate already understands exactly what it's attached to.

## When To Use

- At the start of every discussion call, as the mental structure the rest of the call is organized around
- When a call has jumped ahead of itself — for example, a candidate asking about cost before the recommendation has been made — and needs to be brought back into sequence
- When a discussion call reveals a gap that should have been caught in discovery, and the call needs to pause and go back a step
- When training a newer advisor who tends to let discussion calls wander without a clear shape

## Discussion Flow

A well-structured discussion call generally moves through four stages, in this order:

**1. Reflect discovery back.** Open by summarizing what you understood from the discovery conversation — the goal underneath the stated goal, the constraints that came up, the authorization status that was confirmed. This isn't a recap for its own sake; it's proof the last call mattered, and it gives the candidate a chance to correct anything you got wrong before you build a recommendation on it.

**2. Present a tailored recommendation.** Once the discovery summary is confirmed, present what Surely Placed can offer specifically in light of that candidate's situation — not a general description of the program, but a direct line from what they told you to what you're recommending (see [Presenting Surely Placed](/docs/discussion/presenting-surely-placed)).

**3. Walk through the process.** Once the candidate understands what's being recommended and why, explain what actually happens if they move forward — the shape of the experience, not just the pitch (see [Explaining the Process](/docs/discussion/explaining-the-process)).

**4. Address investment.** Only once the candidate understands what they'd be getting and how it would actually work does a conversation about cost make sense. Raised any earlier, it tends to land as a number floating with no context; raised here, it lands as the cost of something the candidate already understands and, ideally, already wants (see [Discussing Investment](/docs/discussion/discussing-investment)).

Each stage should end with a natural checkpoint — a question that confirms the candidate is actually with you — before moving to the next. Don't treat the four stages as a monologue split into four parts; each one is still a conversation.

## Conversation Examples

**Opening a discussion call with a discovery recap:**

> **Advisor:** "Before I get into anything today, I want to make sure I've got last time right. From what you told me, the real driver for you isn't the title — it's job security, especially after what happened with the layoff. And you're on STEM OPT with about a year left, cap question not relevant since you're not on H-1B yet. Did I get that right, or is there anything you'd change?"
> **Candidate:** "That's pretty much it, yeah — maybe I'd add that I also just want to feel technically sharp again, not just secure."
> **Advisor:** "Good addition, and useful — that actually shapes what I want to walk you through today. Let's start there."

**Catching a call that's jumped ahead of itself:**

> **Candidate:** "So what does this actually cost?"
> **Advisor:** "I'll get you a clear answer on that, I promise — but I want to walk you through what you'd actually be paying for first, because the number on its own won't mean much without that context. Is it okay if we cover that first, and then get straight to cost?"
> **Candidate:** "Yeah, that's fair."

## Decision Points

- IF the candidate confirms the discovery recap without correction →
  THEN proceed directly into the tailored recommendation — the shared understanding is solid enough to build on.
- IF the candidate corrects or adds to the recap →
  THEN incorporate the correction before moving forward, even if it means adjusting the recommendation you'd planned to present.
- IF the recap reveals something that should have been caught in discovery — an authorization detail, a goal that's shifted significantly — →
  THEN pause discussion and address it directly, routing to [Candidate Qualification](/docs/discovery/candidate-qualification) or a focused visa conversation if needed, before continuing.
- IF the candidate asks about cost before the recommendation or process has been covered →
  THEN acknowledge the question honestly, commit to answering it, and ask permission to cover recommendation and process first so the number has context.
- IF a candidate seems disengaged or rushed through the recap stage →
  THEN check in directly rather than pushing forward on a shaky foundation — a discussion call built on an unconfirmed recap tends to unravel later.

## Common Mistakes

- Starting a discussion call as if it's the candidate's first conversation with Surely Placed, instead of building visibly on discovery
- Presenting cost early because the candidate asked, without first securing agreement on what they'd be paying for
- Treating the four-stage structure as a rigid script rather than a sequence that still needs real back-and-forth at each stage
- Moving forward with a recommendation after a discovery recap that the candidate only partially confirmed
- Letting a call wander between the four stages without a clear sense of which one is currently happening

## Things Never To Say

- "We covered that last time, so let's not go over it again." (Skipping the recap because it feels redundant to the advisor removes the exact thing that makes the candidate feel heard — even a short version matters.)
- "Let's just get to the number, since that's what everyone really wants to know." (Undermines the sequence this whole structure depends on, and reduces the conversation to a transaction before it's earned that framing.)
- Any suggestion that discovery findings don't actually shape what gets recommended — the recommendation has to be visibly built on what was learned, not generic regardless of what the candidate said.

## Advanced Advisor Tips

Experienced advisors learn to keep the discovery recap short but specific — a thirty-second summary that includes one or two precise details is more convincing than a longer, vaguer one. Precision is what proves the last call was actually heard; length just proves the advisor took notes.

It's also worth developing comfort with holding a candidate's cost question at bay briefly, without making it feel like a dodge. The line between "I'll get to that, here's why the order matters" and stonewalling is mostly about tone and about actually following through quickly — candidates tolerate a short delay easily as long as they trust it's genuinely coming, and soon.

## Summary

A discussion call works best in a specific order: reflect discovery back, present a tailored recommendation, explain the process, then address investment. Each stage earns credibility for the next, and skipping ahead — especially straight to cost — tends to undercut a conversation that would otherwise land well. Confirm the discovery recap before building on it, and don't be afraid to pause the sequence if something surfaces that needs to be resolved first.
