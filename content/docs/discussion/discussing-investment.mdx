# Discussing Investment

> **Note on pricing:** This article teaches how to have the cost conversation honestly and well. It does not state a specific price, fee, payment structure, or discount anywhere, and it never will — those numbers change, and this document doesn't. Confirm current program cost and payment structure with the authoritative internal pricing resource before every investment conversation. Never quote a figure from memory.

## Purpose

By this point in a well-structured discussion call, the candidate understands what's being recommended and what the process actually involves. This is where cost comes into the conversation — not as an isolated number, but as something now genuinely connected to everything discussed before it. This article is about having that conversation honestly: naming cost directly, tying it to what it actually buys, and treating a candidate's financial hesitation as legitimate information rather than an obstacle to manage.

## Learning Objectives

- Introduce cost directly and without unnecessary hedging, once the earlier stages of the call have earned the right to raise it
- Connect cost explicitly to the specific value discussed earlier in the call, rather than presenting it as a disconnected number
- Respond to financial hesitation by understanding it, not by minimizing it or rushing past it
- Avoid ever stating a specific number, discount, or guarantee not confirmed as current
- Recognize when a cost conversation is really surfacing a bigger, unresolved doubt about the recommendation itself

## Candidate Psychology

Very few candidates hear a cost number in isolation. They hear it against whatever they've already decided the recommendation is worth to them — which is exactly why this conversation belongs after the recommendation and process have been genuinely understood, not before. A number introduced too early gets evaluated against almost nothing; the same number introduced after real understanding gets evaluated against something concrete.

Cost conversations also tend to surface things candidates haven't said out loud yet — a tighter financial situation than they've admitted to, a spouse who isn't fully convinced, real uncertainty about whether they're making the right call at all. It's worth remembering that hesitation at this stage is rarely only about the number. Sometimes the number is simply the number, and sometimes it's the first concrete, unavoidable moment where a bigger, quieter doubt finally has to be addressed.

There's also a specific anxiety worth naming: many candidates in this population have been in a vulnerable financial position before — between jobs, managing a visa timeline, supporting family — and a conversation about spending money on themselves can trigger genuine guilt or self-doubt that has nothing to do with whether the investment is reasonable. An advisor who notices this and responds to the person, not just the budget question, handles this stage far better than one who treats it as purely a numbers conversation.

## Advisor Objective

Introduce cost clearly and directly, tied explicitly to the value already discussed, and respond to any hesitation by understanding what's actually behind it — without ever stating a specific figure that hasn't been confirmed as current, and without treating hesitation as something to be talked past.

## When To Use

- As the fourth and final stage of a structured discussion call, after recommendation and process have both been covered (see [Structuring the Discussion Call](/docs/discussion/structuring-the-discussion-call))
- When a candidate asks about cost earlier than the natural sequence, and needs a direct acknowledgment plus a request to cover it in order (see [Structuring the Discussion Call](/docs/discussion/structuring-the-discussion-call) § Decision Points)
- When a candidate's reaction to cost seems to be about more than the number itself, and the real concern needs to be surfaced before the conversation can move forward honestly

## Discussion Flow

Introduce the topic directly — don't apologize for bringing up cost or bury it in soft language. A brief, clear transition works better than a long lead-in: something like "let's talk about what this actually costs, and what that's tied to."

State plainly that you'll confirm the exact current figure rather than reciting a number from memory if there's any chance it's changed — accuracy here matters more than sounding fluent.

Connect the cost directly back to what was covered in the recommendation and process stages — remind the candidate concretely what it's attached to, rather than letting the number sit on its own.

Ask directly how the candidate is feeling about it, and actually listen to the answer rather than moving straight into handling an objection that hasn't been raised yet.

## Conversation Examples

**Direct introduction, tied to value already discussed:**

> **Advisor:** "Let's talk about what this actually costs, and what it's tied to. I want to give you the accurate, current figure rather than something from memory, so let me confirm that with you precisely — but I do want to frame it against what we just walked through: the readiness work, the employer access, the ongoing support through the process. This isn't a number floating on its own — it's attached to all of that. How are you feeling about it, now that you've seen what it's actually connected to?"
> **Candidate:** "Honestly, better than I expected, now that I understand what's included."
> **Advisor:** "Good to hear — and I'd rather you tell me that honestly than just go along with it. Anything about it that still feels uncertain?"

**Surfacing a deeper concern underneath a cost reaction:**

> **Candidate:** "I mean... that's more than I was expecting, if I'm honest."
> **Advisor:** "I appreciate you saying that directly instead of just going quiet on me. Can I ask — is it the number itself that's the issue, or is it more about where things stand for you financially right now, more broadly?"
> **Candidate:** "...It's more the second thing. I've been out of work for a few months, so anything feels like a lot right now."
> **Advisor:** "That makes complete sense, and it's an important thing to have said out loud. That's actually a different conversation than just negotiating a number — it's about whether the timing is right for you at all. Can we talk through that honestly, rather than me just trying to make the number feel smaller?"

## Decision Points

- IF the candidate responds neutrally or positively once cost is connected to the value discussed →
  THEN move naturally toward next steps, without over-explaining or continuing to sell past a decision that seems settled.
- IF the candidate expresses direct hesitation about the number itself →
  THEN ask what's underneath it before responding — a specific concern about the number's fairness is a different conversation than a broader concern about affordability right now.
- IF the hesitation turns out to be about broader financial timing rather than the number itself →
  THEN treat this honestly as a timing question, not a pricing objection — this may mean the honest answer is "not right now," and that's a legitimate outcome (see the Objection Handling module, planned for a future sprint).
- IF a candidate asks for a discount, waiver, or nonstandard payment arrangement →
  THEN do not invent or promise any adjustment on the call — direct the request to the appropriate current internal process rather than answering from assumption.
- IF cost hesitation seems to be masking doubt about the recommendation itself, not the money →
  THEN go back and check whether the recommendation genuinely landed, rather than continuing to discuss cost as if that's the real issue.

## Common Mistakes

- Introducing cost apologetically, as though it's an uncomfortable thing to bring up rather than a normal part of an honest conversation
- Reciting a number from memory instead of confirming it's current before stating it to a candidate
- Responding to hesitation by immediately trying to justify the number harder, instead of asking what's actually behind the reaction
- Treating every instance of hesitation as a pricing objection, when it's sometimes a timing or confidence issue wearing a pricing costume
- Promising or implying a discount, waiver, or special arrangement without confirming it through the proper internal process

## Things Never To Say

- Any specific dollar figure, discount, or payment term not freshly confirmed as current — see the standing note at the top of this article.
- "It's an investment in yourself, so it's always worth it." (A blanket claim that dismisses a candidate's real, individual financial situation instead of engaging with it honestly.)
- "A lot of people find a way to make it work." (Vague social pressure that sidesteps the candidate's actual concern rather than addressing it directly.)

## Advanced Advisor Tips

Experienced advisors learn to sit with a candidate's silence after stating cost, the same way they would after any other direct ask — resisting the urge to immediately soften the number or start negotiating against an objection that hasn't actually been raised yet. Often the candidate just needs a moment to process, not a response.

It's also worth developing the instinct to separate "is this a fair number for what's included" from "is now the right time for me financially" as two genuinely different conversations, even though they can sound identical in the first sentence a candidate says. Responding to the wrong one wastes the candidate's patience and doesn't resolve the real concern.

## Summary

Discuss cost directly, once the recommendation and process have earned the right to raise it, and always tie the number to the specific value already discussed rather than presenting it in isolation. Confirm figures rather than reciting them from memory, and when hesitation shows up, find out honestly whether it's about the number, the timing, or a deeper doubt about the recommendation itself — each of those deserves a different, honest response, not a single script applied to all three.
