# Presenting Surely Placed

## Purpose

This is the moment in the call where the advisor stops gathering information and starts making a case — specifically, a case for why Surely Placed fits *this* candidate's situation. This article is not about what Surely Placed offers in the abstract; it's about how to present it so it sounds like a direct answer to what the candidate said in discovery, rather than a pitch that would sound identical to any candidate on any call.

The distinction matters more than it might seem. Two candidates can hear the exact same facts about Surely Placed and come away with completely different impressions, depending on whether those facts were connected to their own situation or delivered as a generic overview. This article is about doing the former, every time.

## Learning Objectives

- Build a recommendation that opens with the candidate's own stated need, not with a description of the program
- Distinguish between explaining what Surely Placed does and explaining why it matters for this specific candidate
- Present Surely Placed honestly, without reaching for language that implies outcomes that can't be promised
- Recognize when a candidate's reaction signals the recommendation didn't land, and adjust in the moment
- Keep the presentation grounded in the candidate's own words wherever possible

## Candidate Psychology

Most candidates have sat through a generic pitch before, and they can usually tell within the first sentence or two whether they're hearing one again. A pitch that opens with "Surely Placed is a program that helps IT professionals..." sounds like it was written before the candidate's discovery call ever happened — because, functionally, it was. Candidates notice this, even if they don't say so directly, and it quietly resets their expectations for the rest of the conversation downward.

What candidates are actually listening for, whether they'd phrase it this way or not, is evidence that the advisor was paying attention. A recommendation that opens with something specific to them — "given that you told me the real issue is feeling technically behind, not just needing a new title" — does more to build credibility in the first ten seconds than almost anything else in the entire call.

There's also a quieter test running underneath this moment: candidates are watching for overreach. Given how many programs and recruiters oversell in this space, a candidate who hears a recommendation that sounds too smooth, too confident, too free of any acknowledged limitation, will often trust it less, not more. A recommendation that's honest about what it can and can't promise tends to land better than one that sounds airtight.

## Advisor Objective

Present Surely Placed as a direct response to what this specific candidate said in discovery, using their own language where possible, and describe it honestly — in terms of what the program provides, not in terms of outcomes that can't be guaranteed.

## When To Use

- Immediately after a confirmed discovery recap, as the second stage of a structured discussion call (see [Structuring the Discussion Call](/docs/discussion/structuring-the-discussion-call))
- When a candidate asks directly, "so what do you guys actually do," and a real answer — not a brochure answer — is needed
- When re-presenting Surely Placed to a candidate whose situation has changed since an earlier conversation, and the recommendation needs to be updated rather than repeated verbatim

## Discussion Flow

Start by naming the specific need you're responding to, in the candidate's own terms if you can manage it — this should take one or two sentences, not a preamble.

Move directly into what Surely Placed provides that addresses that specific need: structured preparation, an employer network relevant to the candidate's target roles, ongoing support through the job search, and whatever else genuinely applies — described plainly, without adjectives doing work that facts should be doing.

Be explicit and matter-of-fact about what the program is not. It is not a guarantee of a job offer, and it is not a substitute for the candidate's own effort and engagement. Say this clearly, not as a legal disclaimer tacked onto the end, but as a genuine part of setting expectations correctly.

Close this stage by checking whether what you've described actually matches what the candidate expected, or is looking for — this is the checkpoint before moving into [Explaining the Process](/docs/discussion/explaining-the-process).

## Conversation Examples

**Tailored opening, grounded in discovery:**

> **Advisor:** "So, based on what you told me last time — that the real thing you're chasing isn't just a new title, it's feeling technically sharp and secure again — here's how I'd frame what we do for someone in your position. The program is built around structured preparation and an employer network specifically in IT roles across the US, Canada, and UK, aimed at getting you in front of the right roles with real readiness behind you, not just a resume. It's not a guarantee of an offer — nobody can honestly promise you that — but it's built to put you in a genuinely stronger position than searching on your own."
> **Candidate:** "Okay, that actually makes sense given what I told you. What does 'structured preparation' actually mean, though?"
> **Advisor:** "Good question — let's get into exactly what that looks like."

**Recovering from a recommendation that didn't land:**

> **Advisor:** "...so given the job security piece you mentioned, that's really what this is built around."
> **Candidate:** "I mean, I guess, but that kind of sounds like what every program says."
> **Advisor:** "Fair pushback, and I don't want to just repeat myself more confidently and hope that lands differently. Let me ask instead — what would actually distinguish a real answer from a generic one, for you? What are you specifically trying to figure out that I haven't addressed yet?"
> **Candidate:** "I guess... how is this actually different from just applying on my own, more aggressively?"
> **Advisor:** "That's the real question, and a fair one. Let's get specific about that instead of me talking in generalities."

## Decision Points

- IF the candidate confirms the recommendation matches what they expected →
  THEN move into [Explaining the Process](/docs/discussion/explaining-the-process) with the recommendation as shared, confirmed ground.
- IF the candidate reacts with skepticism or says it "sounds generic" →
  THEN don't repeat the same framing more forcefully — ask directly what would actually distinguish a real answer, and let their response guide what to cover next.
- IF the candidate's discovery situation has shifted since the last call (new job, changed timeline, new constraint) →
  THEN update the recommendation to reflect the shift before continuing — do not present a recommendation built on outdated discovery information.
- IF a candidate asks a specific question about outcomes ("will this get me a job") mid-presentation →
  THEN answer honestly and directly — describe what the program provides, and be clear that no outcome can be guaranteed, rather than deflecting the question.

## Common Mistakes

- Opening the presentation with a description of Surely Placed instead of the candidate's own stated need
- Reusing the same phrasing across candidates regardless of what came up in their individual discovery calls
- Letting enthusiasm slide into language that implies a guaranteed outcome, even unintentionally
- Treating candidate skepticism as something to talk past rather than something to directly address
- Presenting the program's limitations as an afterthought instead of stating them plainly as part of honest framing

## Things Never To Say

- "This basically guarantees you'll land something." (No program can guarantee an individual outcome. Describe what's provided, not a promised result.)
- Any invented statistic about past candidate outcomes, placement rates, or timelines — these do not appear anywhere in this presentation because they are not something an advisor states from memory or invents in the moment.
- "Everyone who does this program ends up better off." (An unfounded, sweeping claim — describe the specific value for this candidate instead of a generalized outcome claim about everyone.)

## Advanced Advisor Tips

Experienced advisors learn to build a small mental library of the exact phrases candidates used during discovery, and to reuse those phrases — not paraphrased, but close to verbatim — when presenting the recommendation. Hearing your own words reflected back is one of the fastest ways a candidate registers that they were actually heard.

It's also worth practicing how to hold the honest limitation ("this isn't a guarantee") in the same breath as genuine confidence in the program's value, without either one undercutting the other. The two aren't in tension. A recommendation that's both confident and honest about its limits is, consistently, more persuasive than one that's confident and silent about them.

## Summary

Presenting Surely Placed well means starting from the candidate's own stated need, not from a general description of the program, and describing what's actually provided in plain terms — preparation, network, support — without ever implying a guaranteed outcome. When a recommendation sounds generic to the candidate, don't repeat it more confidently; ask what would actually distinguish a real answer, and follow their lead.
