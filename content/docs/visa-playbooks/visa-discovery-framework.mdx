# Visa Discovery Framework

> **This module is a sales playbook, not immigration guidance.** Every article in the Visa Playbooks module teaches an Admissions Advisor how to understand a candidate's mindset, urgency, and discovery needs for a given work authorization category — not how to interpret immigration law. Advisors are never in a position to confirm eligibility, interpret rules, or predict a government agency's decision. Any question that requires that kind of interpretation gets the same answer, every time: it goes to a qualified immigration professional, not to the advisor's best guess.

## Purpose

Every visa or work authorization category a candidate might hold shapes the sales conversation differently — how much urgency is real, what kind of risk the candidate is managing, and what discovery needs to happen before a recommendation makes sense. This article is the foundation the rest of the module builds on: the general approach to visa-related discovery that applies regardless of category, and the standard every category-specific playbook in this module follows.

## Candidate Profile

This article doesn't describe one candidate profile — it describes the discovery approach that applies before a specific profile is even identified. Every candidate who reaches a discovery call has some form of work authorization, per Surely Placed's basic eligibility gate (see [Candidate Qualification](/docs/discovery/candidate-qualification)), but the category, timeline, and how tied it is to a specific employer vary enormously, and none of that should be assumed from context.

## Typical Mindset

Across nearly every category, candidates carry more anxiety about their work authorization than they show in the first few minutes of a call. Status can affect their ability to remain in a country they've built a life in, which makes this one of the most sensitive topics in any conversation Surely Placed has with a candidate — regardless of which specific category applies.

## Buying Motivation

Work authorization category shapes buying motivation indirectly but significantly: a candidate managing a narrowing timeline is often motivated by urgency, while a candidate with a more stable or independent status is often motivated by long-term positioning rather than immediate necessity. Neither motivation is better or worse — they call for different pacing, not different levels of seriousness.

## Common Pain Points

- Uncertainty about their own status, sometimes because they genuinely don't know the specifics, and sometimes because they've received conflicting information from different sources
- Fear that disclosing status complexity will make them seem like a harder case an advisor won't want to invest time in
- Confusion about how their category affects what kind of employer or role is realistic
- Prior experience with a recruiter or "consultant" who spoke confidently and inaccurately about their situation

## Discovery Questions

1. "What's your current work authorization status, in your own words?"
2. "Do you know how much time is remaining on your current authorization, or when it's set to expire or need renewal?"
3. "Is your authorization tied to a specific employer, or is it independent of who you work for?"
4. "Have you worked with an immigration attorney on this, or is this mostly self-researched so far?"
5. "Is there anything about your status that feels uncertain or unresolved to you right now?"

## Qualification Signals

- The candidate can describe their category and general timeline clearly, even if they don't know every detail
- The candidate has documentation (an approval notice, a card, an official letter) they can refer to, even if they haven't memorized every date
- The candidate's description of their situation stays consistent as they answer more questions, rather than shifting in ways that suggest guesswork

## Risk Signals

- The candidate uses a status name that doesn't clearly map to a real category, or uses several different terms interchangeably
- The candidate expresses genuine uncertainty about whether they're currently authorized to work at all
- The candidate describes a status that sounds like it may have lapsed, been denied, or is otherwise in question

See [Visa Red Flags](/docs/visa-playbooks/visa-red-flags) for the full list of signals that mean a conversation should pause and route to qualified immigration counsel before continuing.

## Presentation Strategy

Never present a recommendation, track, or timeline that depends on an assumption about the candidate's status. If a status detail is unclear, resolve it — or flag it for the candidate to resolve with counsel — before building anything on top of it.

## Objection Patterns

Objections rooted in a genuine status question are not objections to be argued with — they're accurate signals that something needs to be confirmed before the conversation can honestly continue. See the category-specific playbooks in this module for the objection patterns typical of each visa type.

## Closing Strategy

No closing conversation should proceed around an open, unresolved question about work authorization. If one surfaces at any point — during discovery, discussion, or closing — pause and resolve it, or route it to qualified counsel, before continuing.

## Advisor Mistakes

- Treating "work authorization" as a single, interchangeable category instead of asking which specific one applies
- Reassuring a candidate about a status question without any basis to know the answer
- Letting a detailed program conversation continue after a candidate has revealed genuine uncertainty about their own status
- Guessing at an eligibility answer to avoid an awkward "I don't know" moment

## Key Takeaways

Every category-specific playbook in this module operates under the same standard: understand the candidate's mindset and discovery needs, never interpret immigration law, and route any rule-based question to qualified counsel rather than guessing. Ask directly, take documentation over assumption, and treat genuine uncertainty as a signal to pause, not a detail to smooth over.
