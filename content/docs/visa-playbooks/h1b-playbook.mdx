# H-1B Playbook

> This is a sales playbook, not immigration guidance, and it follows the shared standard laid out in [Visa Discovery Framework](/docs/visa-playbooks/visa-discovery-framework). Cap rules, filing requirements, and eligibility criteria are deliberately left out of this article; those belong to the candidate's own documentation and to a qualified immigration attorney, not to an advisor's recollection.

## Candidate Profile

Candidates in this category are currently employed on H-1B status, sponsored by a current employer, and are often exploring whether a move to a different role or employer is realistic. Compared to earlier-stage categories, these candidates typically have more established professional experience and a status that is more stable in the short term, but tied to their current employer's sponsorship.

## Typical Mindset

Many H-1B candidates approach a career move with more caution than their experience level might suggest, because status is tied to their current employer and any change carries some degree of process and uncertainty. This often produces a pattern worth recognizing directly: real, sometimes long-standing frustration with a current role or employer, held back by risk-aversion tied to status.

## Buying Motivation

The dominant motivation is usually a ceiling that's been reached — being underpaid, under-titled, or under-challenged relative to actual capability, often after years of proving themselves specifically because their employer knows status creates some leverage. A meaningful secondary motivation is simply wanting to be recognized for work already being done, not just a bigger opportunity in the abstract.

## Common Pain Points

- Uncertainty about how a change of employer would actually work for their specific situation, and hesitation to explore it without a clear picture first
- Risk-aversion built up over years, sometimes disproportionate to the candidate's actual situation
- Difficulty finding employers who are known to be realistic about supporting a transfer, versus those who simply list a role without a clear track record
- A sense that their current employer is aware of their limited options and may be leveraging that awareness, whether or not that's explicitly stated

## Discovery Questions

1. "Are you currently on H-1B, sponsored by your current employer?"
2. "How long have you been with your current employer, and how would you describe the relationship — growth issue, comfort issue, or something more urgent?"
3. "Have you looked into how a transfer to a new employer would actually work, or is that part of what you're hoping to understand today?"
4. "Is there anything time-sensitive on your end that I should know about?"
5. "What's actually pulling you toward a change — the type of work, the compensation, the company, or something about where you are right now?"

## Qualification Signals

- The candidate can describe their current role and employer relationship with real specificity
- The candidate is asking practical, forward-looking questions about how a transition would work, rather than expressing only general dissatisfaction
- The candidate's stated motivation is specific and grounded (a particular ceiling reached, a particular frustration) rather than vague restlessness

## Risk Signals

- The candidate is unsure of basic facts about their own H-1B situation, including whether their current employer is aware they're exploring options
- The candidate describes a status detail that sounds unusual or inconsistent without a clear explanation
- The candidate's timeline pressure seems to be driven by something they haven't fully disclosed yet (an ultimatum, a notice period, a status concern)

If any of these come up, pause and see [Visa Red Flags](/docs/visa-playbooks/visa-red-flags) before continuing the sales conversation. A fuller discovery pattern specific to a transfer scenario — *Discovery Call: The H-1B Transfer Candidate Seeking a Stronger Employer* (`discovery-h1b-transfer-stronger-employer`) — is planned for this repository but not yet published here.

## Presentation Strategy

Separate the emotional driver (frustration, feeling undervalued) from the practical target (role type, employer type) early, and build the recommendation around both. Candidates in this category respond well to specificity about how a transition process generally works and poorly to vague reassurance that "it'll be fine."

## Objection Patterns

Risk-aversion built up over time can surface as a version of [Need Time to Think](/docs/objections/need-time-to-think) that's really about status risk rather than program fit — ask directly rather than assuming. Candidates who've been underpaid for years may also raise a sharper version of [Price Objection](/docs/objections/price-objection), since they're often acutely aware of what they've already sacrificed financially.

## Closing Strategy

Confirm the candidate has a realistic, personally-verified understanding of how a transfer would work for their specific situation before closing — this is not something the advisor asserts. If real status uncertainty exists, resolve it or route to counsel before finalizing any recommendation tied to a change of employer.

## Advisor Mistakes

- Assuming every H-1B candidate has the same risk profile or timeline, when individual circumstances vary significantly
- Promising or implying that a transfer to a new employer is straightforward or guaranteed
- Missing the emotional driver underneath a candidate's stated goal and only addressing the practical target
- Failing to ask directly whether something time-sensitive or undisclosed is shaping the candidate's urgency

## Key Takeaways

H-1B candidates exploring a change of employer are often managing real frustration held back by real risk-aversion — treat both as legitimate, and separate the emotional driver from the practical target before building a recommendation. Never assert how a transfer would work for a specific candidate; that determination belongs to the candidate's own research and qualified immigration counsel.
