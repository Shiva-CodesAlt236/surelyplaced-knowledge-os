# H-4 EAD Playbook

> This is a sales playbook, not immigration guidance, built on the shared standard in [Visa Discovery Framework](/docs/visa-playbooks/visa-discovery-framework). Eligibility rules for this category have shifted over time, so an advisor stating them from memory is not a safe default — the candidate's own documentation and their own immigration counsel are the only reliable source.

## Candidate Profile

Candidates in this category hold employment authorization as a dependent of an H-1B holder, most often a spouse. Many are re-entering the workforce after a gap, or entering the workforce in this country for the first time, sometimes after having a well-established career elsewhere before relocating.

## Typical Mindset

This category carries a distinct emotional layer that's worth naming directly: many candidates have paused or restarted a career they'd already built, and the conversation is often as much about professional identity as it is about logistics. There can also be a quieter awareness that this work authorization category is tied to someone else's status, which sits differently than a category tied to the candidate's own standing.

## Buying Motivation

Motivation is frequently a mix of financial contribution and personal fulfillment — wanting to be a working professional again, not just a dependent on paper. For candidates who had strong careers before relocating, there's often a specific desire to be taken seriously at a level that reflects their real experience, not treated as an entry-level candidate by default.

## Common Pain Points

- A gap in local work history that can be hard to explain to employers, even when the underlying skills and experience are strong
- A sense of starting over professionally, sometimes after years of established seniority elsewhere
- Uncertainty about how their category is perceived by employers, and whether it will raise questions during a hiring process
- Feeling like their career is secondary to their spouse's, even when that's not how they see themselves

## Discovery Questions

1. "What's your current work authorization status, and how long have you had it?"
2. "What did your career look like before this move — what level and type of role?"
3. "Have you worked in this country before, or would this be your first role here?"
4. "How do you feel your work authorization category is likely to come up in a hiring conversation, if at all?"
5. "What would it mean to you, beyond the practical side, to be working again?"

## Qualification Signals

- The candidate can speak specifically and confidently about their prior career experience, even after a gap
- The candidate has clarity about their current authorization category and has documentation to reference
- The candidate is thinking concretely about role level and fit, not just "any job to get back into it"

## Risk Signals

- The candidate is unsure whether their current authorization is still valid or has expired
- The candidate describes their authorization as tied to a spouse's status that has itself changed recently (a job loss, a status change) without having confirmed what that means for their own authorization
- The candidate seems to be relying on outdated information about this category from when they first received it

If any of these come up, pause and see [Visa Red Flags](/docs/visa-playbooks/visa-red-flags) before continuing the sales conversation.

## Presentation Strategy

Lead with respect for the candidate's prior career level — avoid any framing that implicitly treats this as an entry-level restart unless the candidate's own background suggests that's accurate. Be specific about how the program can help translate prior experience into a credible narrative for employers in this market, rather than a generic pitch about "getting back into the workforce."

## Objection Patterns

Candidates in this category sometimes carry heightened skepticism if they've felt overlooked or misunderstood by employers before — see [Trust and Credibility](/docs/objections/trust-and-credibility). Family decision-making dynamics are also common here, particularly when a spouse's career or status is part of the household's broader planning — see [Parents & Spouse Approval](/docs/objections/parents-spouse-approval).

## Closing Strategy

Confirm the candidate's authorization is current and hasn't been affected by any recent change in their spouse's status — from their own documentation, not from the advisor's assumption — before closing. Given the identity dimension often present in this category, make sure the close reflects genuine readiness, not just logistical agreement.

## Advisor Mistakes

- Treating this category as automatically lower-priority or entry-level without asking about the candidate's actual background
- Assuming continuity of authorization without checking whether anything about the sponsoring spouse's status has recently changed
- Missing the identity and confidence dimension of this conversation by focusing only on logistics
- Speculating about how this category will be perceived by a specific employer instead of being honest about what can't be predicted

## Key Takeaways

Candidates on H-4 dependent work authorization are often navigating a career restart with real professional history behind them — treat that history with the seriousness it deserves, and don't default to entry-level framing. Never assert this category's current rules or eligibility; confirm status against the candidate's own documentation, especially if anything about the sponsoring spouse's status has recently changed, and route uncertainty to qualified immigration counsel.
