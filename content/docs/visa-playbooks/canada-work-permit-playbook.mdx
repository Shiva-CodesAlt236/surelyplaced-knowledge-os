# Canada Work Permit Playbook

> This is a sales playbook, not immigration guidance — the shared standard is defined in [Visa Discovery Framework](/docs/visa-playbooks/visa-discovery-framework). Canadian work permits vary widely in type and structure, so never assume which kind a candidate holds; treat their own documentation and their own immigration adviser as the only real authority on the specifics.

## Candidate Profile

Candidates in this category hold a Canadian work permit, which can take several different forms — some tied to a specific employer, some open and not tied to one, and some connected to a post-graduation pathway. The specific type shapes the conversation significantly, and it should always be confirmed directly rather than assumed from how the candidate initially describes it.

## Typical Mindset

Many candidates in this category are thinking about their work permit not just as current authorization, but as a stage in a longer path toward permanent residency. That framing means the job search is often evaluated not just on the role itself, but on how it might support a longer-term goal, even when the candidate hasn't fully mapped out how.

## Buying Motivation

Motivation frequently blends immediate career goals with long-term settlement goals — a candidate may be as focused on finding a role and employer that supports their broader path to permanent residency as they are on the role itself. For candidates on an employer-tied permit, there's often specific interest in employers known to be supportive of long-term plans.

## Common Pain Points

- Uncertainty about how a given role or employer might affect a longer-term permanent residency path, without a confident answer
- For employer-tied permit holders, similar risk-aversion to what shows up in employer-tied US categories, tied to the specific employer relationship
- Difficulty finding clear, trustworthy guidance on how career decisions intersect with immigration planning, especially from sources that aren't formal counsel
- Sometimes, comparing options between Canada and other countries, without a clear framework for that comparison

## Discovery Questions

1. "What type of work permit do you currently hold, in your own words?"
2. "Is your permit tied to a specific employer, or is it open?"
3. "How does this job search fit into your broader plans — are you thinking about permanent residency, or is this more about the role itself right now?"
4. "Have you worked with an immigration consultant or lawyer on your broader plans?"
5. "Is there anything about your permit or timeline that feels unclear to you?"

## Qualification Signals

- The candidate can describe their permit type with reasonable clarity, even if they defer exact details to their documentation
- The candidate has a coherent sense of how this search fits into their broader goals, even if the specifics are still forming
- The candidate is asking practical questions about employer fit rather than expressing general confusion about their whole situation

## Risk Signals

- The candidate is unsure what type of permit they hold or whether it's still valid
- The candidate describes a plan that assumes a specific outcome for a future immigration process, without having confirmed that assumption with counsel
- The candidate seems to be relying on informal, secondhand information about how their situation works

If any of these come up, pause and see [Visa Red Flags](/docs/visa-playbooks/visa-red-flags) before continuing the sales conversation.

## Presentation Strategy

Connect the recommendation to the candidate's broader goals when they've articulated them, without ever speculating about how a specific role or employer will affect a longer-term immigration outcome. Be honest when a question crosses into territory that requires their own counsel rather than program guidance.

## Objection Patterns

Candidates weighing Canada against other countries may raise questions connected to [Trust and Credibility](/docs/objections/trust-and-credibility) if they're comparing multiple programs or advisors across markets. Employer-tied permit holders may raise hesitation similar to what shows up in [H-1B Playbook](/docs/visa-playbooks/h1b-playbook), rooted in employer relationship risk rather than program fit.

## Closing Strategy

Confirm the candidate's permit type and any employer dependency directly before closing on a recommendation tied to a change of employer. If the candidate's broader plans depend on an assumption about how this fits into a longer-term process, encourage them to confirm that with their own counsel rather than treating the advisor's understanding as sufficient.

## Advisor Mistakes

- Assuming a specific permit type without confirming it directly
- Speculating about how a given job or employer will affect a candidate's permanent residency prospects
- Treating every Canadian work permit holder as having the same level of employer dependency, when this varies significantly by permit type
- Failing to distinguish between career-focused motivation and immigration-planning motivation when they're actually different for a given candidate

## Key Takeaways

Canadian work permits vary significantly in type and in how tied they are to a specific employer — always confirm which applies rather than assuming. Many candidates in this category are evaluating their job search against a longer-term permanent residency goal; support that framing without ever speculating about how a specific decision will affect an immigration outcome, and route that question to qualified counsel.
