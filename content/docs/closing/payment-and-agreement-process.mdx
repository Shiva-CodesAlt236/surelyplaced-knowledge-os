# Payment & Agreement Process

> **Honesty note:** This repository does not yet contain Surely Placed's specific pricing, payment terms, contract or agreement language, refund policy, or enrollment paperwork process. Rather than invent plausible-sounding steps to fill that gap, this article states that gap plainly and teaches the part that doesn't depend on it: how an advisor should communicate during this stage. Once the authoritative payment and agreement details exist, this article should be updated to reference them directly — it should not be used as a substitute for that documentation.

## Purpose

Once a candidate says yes, the conversation shifts from a sales conversation to an operational one — reviewing what they're agreeing to and moving into payment. This is a moment where trust is either reinforced or quietly damaged, depending on how clearly and honestly the advisor handles it. This article covers the communication standard for that moment. It does not describe Surely Placed's specific payment steps, contract terms, or policies, because those aren't yet documented anywhere in this repository, and stating them here from assumption would risk telling a candidate something inaccurate.

## Learning Objectives

- Recognize what this article can and cannot tell you, and why that distinction matters
- Communicate clearly with a candidate during the agreement and payment stage without inventing specifics
- Know where to direct candidate questions about exact terms, pricing, or policy
- Avoid stating anything about payment or agreement terms that hasn't been confirmed as current and accurate
- Keep a candidate's trust intact during a stage where vagueness or guessing would damage it

## When To Use

- Immediately after a candidate says yes in a closing call (see [Decision to Enrollment](/docs/closing/decision-to-enrollment))
- When a candidate asks a specific question about payment terms, contract details, or policy that this article does not answer
- When training a newer advisor on how to handle this stage professionally before the underlying policy documentation exists

## Conversation Framework

Confirm the decision plainly before moving into logistics — a short, genuine acknowledgment that the candidate has made a real decision, not a rushed pivot straight into paperwork.

Tell the candidate honestly what you can and can't confirm on the call itself. If exact payment terms, timing, or agreement language need to come from a different resource or a follow-up step, say so directly rather than approximating.

Direct any specific question about pricing, payment structure, refund terms, or contract language to the correct current resource or next step — do not answer from memory or assumption.

Confirm what happens next in terms you're actually sure of: for example, that the candidate will receive something in writing, or that a specific person or process will follow up — only state this if you know it to be true, and if you don't, say that plainly instead.

## Best Practices

- Be as specific as you can about what you know, and equally clear about what you don't.
- Never fill a gap in your own knowledge with something that sounds reasonable but hasn't been confirmed.
- If a candidate asks a question you can't answer accurately, say so directly and tell them how they'll get a real answer, rather than deflecting or guessing.
- Keep this stage calm and unhurried — a candidate who just made a real decision deserves a clear, steady handoff, not a rushed one.
- Treat any written confirmation or agreement the candidate receives as the authoritative source, and encourage them to read it carefully rather than relying only on what was said on the call.

## Common Mistakes

- Inventing or guessing at payment amounts, timelines, or terms to avoid an awkward "I'm not sure" moment
- Describing a contract or refund policy from general assumption about how "these things usually work"
- Rushing through this stage because the emotional high point of the call already happened at the ask
- Failing to tell a candidate clearly what happens next, leaving them uncertain right after making a real commitment
- Treating a candidate's specific policy question as unimportant because the "real" conversation already happened

## Examples

**Honest handling of a question outside what the advisor can confirm:**

> **Advisor:** "Congratulations on the decision — I know that wasn't a small one. I want to be straightforward with you about what happens next: I can walk you through the general shape of it, but for the exact payment terms and agreement details, I want to make sure you get the accurate, current version rather than me reciting something from memory."
> **Candidate:** "Okay, that's fair. What happens next, then?"
> **Advisor:** "I'm going to make sure you get that information directly and accurately rather than guess at it here. What I can tell you for certain is [only state what you actually know to be true at this point]. Does that work for you as a next step?"

**A specific policy question the advisor doesn't have the answer to:**

> **Candidate:** "What's your refund policy if this doesn't work out for me?"
> **Advisor:** "That's a fair and important question, and I want to give you an accurate answer rather than an approximate one — I don't want to tell you something today that turns out not to match the actual terms. Let me make sure you get that in writing, directly, rather than guess at it on this call."

## Key Takeaways

This stage matters as much for what an advisor is honest about not knowing as for what they can confirm. Don't fill a documentation gap with an invented policy, price, or term — state plainly what you know, direct specific questions to the accurate current source, and give the candidate a clear, honest sense of what happens next.
