# Decision to Enrollment

## Purpose

By the time a candidate reaches a closing call, discovery has confirmed they're a real fit and discussion has walked them through a tailored recommendation, the process, and the cost. This article covers what happens next — the shape of the closing call itself, and how it moves a candidate from "I understand this" to an actual decision, one way or the other. A closing call isn't a separate persuasion event bolted onto the end of the funnel. It's the natural conclusion of everything that came before it, and it should feel that way to the candidate.

## Learning Objectives

- Describe the general shape of a closing call and how it differs from a discussion call
- Recognize when a candidate is genuinely ready for a closing conversation versus being pushed into one early
- Sequence the closing call so the ask comes after readiness is actually confirmed, not before
- Know what to do when a closing call surfaces something that should have been resolved earlier in the funnel
- Treat both "yes" and "not now" as legitimate, complete outcomes of a well-run closing call

## When To Use

- After a discussion call has been completed and confirmed — recommendation, process, and investment are all understood (see the Discussion Calls module)
- When a candidate signals readiness on their own ("so what happens if I want to move forward") and a closing conversation should follow naturally
- When scheduling a dedicated closing call after a discussion call ended with real, but not yet fully resolved, hesitation

## Conversation Framework

A closing call generally has three parts, in this order:

**1. Confirm readiness.** Before asking for anything, check where the candidate actually stands. This isn't a formality — it tells you whether this call should move toward a direct ask, or whether something needs to be addressed first. Asking "where are you at with this?" and listening carefully to the answer is worth more here than any amount of enthusiasm.

**2. Ask directly.** Once readiness is confirmed, or once you've checked and there's no new information to work through, ask plainly whether the candidate wants to move forward. Don't hedge the ask into vagueness, and don't fill the silence that follows it. (See [Asking for the Commitment](/docs/closing/asking-for-the-commitment).)

**3. Respond to the real answer.** A "yes" moves into the agreement and payment conversation. A specific hesitation gets addressed directly, on its own terms. A genuine "not now" gets respected, with a clear, specific plan for what happens next. (See [Handling Final Hesitation](/docs/closing/handling-final-hesitation) and [Payment & Agreement Process](/docs/closing/payment-and-agreement-process).)

If a closing call surfaces something that should have come up earlier — an authorization detail that was never fully confirmed, a goal that's shifted significantly, a recommendation the candidate never really agreed with — pause and address that directly rather than pushing forward on a shaky foundation. A closing call is not the place to first discover that discovery wasn't finished.

## Best Practices

- Let the candidate's own words set the pace of the call — a candidate who sounds ready doesn't need to be walked through a longer runway than they need.
- Keep the "confirm readiness" step genuinely open-ended. A closed question here ("are you ready to sign up today?") skips past information you actually need.
- Treat the ask as a single, clear moment, not something to work up to gradually across the whole call.
- Hold space after the ask. The next words belong to the candidate, not the advisor.
- If a closing call needs to be split across two conversations because something real came up, say so plainly and schedule a specific follow-up rather than leaving it open-ended.

## Common Mistakes

- Skipping the readiness check and moving straight to the ask because the call was scheduled as a "closing call"
- Treating a candidate's hesitation about a leftover discussion-stage question as a closing objection when it's actually unfinished business from an earlier call
- Filling the silence after the ask because it feels uncomfortable, rather than letting the candidate think
- Rushing a candidate toward same-call enrollment when they've clearly named a missing decision-maker or an unresolved concern
- Treating "not now" as a failed call instead of a legitimate, complete outcome

## Examples

**A closing call that opens with a genuine readiness check:**

> **Advisor:** "Before I ask you anything, I want to check in — where are you at with this, after everything we've talked through?"
> **Candidate:** "Honestly, I think I'm ready. I've thought about the cost, I understand the process, I don't have any open questions."
> **Advisor:** "Good to hear, and I believe you — you've been thorough about this the whole way through. So let's not overcomplicate it: do you want to move forward?"
> **Candidate:** "Yeah. Let's do it."

**A closing call that surfaces unfinished business from discussion:**

> **Advisor:** "Where are you at with this?"
> **Candidate:** "Honestly, I'm still not totally sure the recommendation fits — I don't think we fully landed on that last time."
> **Advisor:** "I appreciate you telling me that directly, and I'd rather pause here than push forward on something you're not actually sure about. Let's go back to the recommendation itself before we talk about anything else today."

## Key Takeaways

A closing call works when it's treated as the conclusion of a process that already earned it, not a separate persuasion event. Confirm readiness honestly before asking for anything, make the ask itself clear and direct, and respond to whatever real answer comes back — including a genuine "not now" — without pressure. If something surfaces that should have been resolved earlier, pause and go back rather than closing over a gap.
