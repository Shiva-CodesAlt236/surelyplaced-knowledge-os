# Candidate Notes Standards

> This is a sales operations playbook. It covers the discipline of writing good notes, not the specific system or fields they're entered into — see [CRM Workflow](/docs/sales-operations/crm-workflow) for why that detail is Operational Policy Pending.

## Purpose

A note written after a call is often the only record another person — a manager, a teammate, or the same advisor weeks later — has of what actually happened. This article covers what makes a note genuinely useful: accurate, specific, and honest about both what went well and what didn't.

## Learning Objectives

- Write notes that reflect what actually happened in a call, not a summary shaped by hoped-for outcomes
- Include the specific details that make a note useful for future preparation
- Recognize what belongs in a note versus what should be escalated separately
- Write notes promptly enough that details aren't lost to memory

## When to Use

- Immediately after any candidate call, while details are still fresh
- When updating a candidate's status or stage, to explain the reasoning behind it
- When handing a candidate off to another advisor, so the notes alone can bring them up to speed

## Operational Context

This article assumes notes are entered into whatever system Surely Placed's actual CRM process specifies — the system and fields themselves are Operational Policy Pending (see [CRM Workflow](/docs/sales-operations/crm-workflow)). What's covered here is the writing discipline itself, which holds regardless of the specific tool.

## Recommended Process

Write the note as close to the call as possible, while the specific details — what the candidate said, how they reacted, what was left unresolved — are still accurately remembered rather than reconstructed later from a vague impression.

Record what actually happened, including hesitation, unresolved objections, or a call that didn't go as planned, rather than only the positive parts. A note that only records good news isn't useful to whoever prepares for the next call using it.

Be specific rather than generic — "candidate raised a comparison objection against another consultancy, wants to think about pricing structure" is far more useful than "had a good call, candidate is interested."

Separate what belongs in a note from what needs active escalation — a note documents; it doesn't substitute for actually raising a serious concern with a manager (see [Internal Escalation Guidelines](/docs/sales-operations/internal-escalation-guidelines)).

## Advisor Responsibilities

- Write notes promptly and specifically after each candidate interaction
- Record what actually happened, including outcomes that weren't ideal
- Use notes to support continuity for whoever picks up the candidate next, including a future version of the same advisor
- Escalate serious concerns directly rather than only noting them and hoping they're seen

## Quality Standards

A good note should let another advisor walk into the next call with that candidate and pick up naturally, without needing to ask the original advisor what actually happened. If a note leaves out something that would matter to whoever reads it next, it isn't complete yet.

## Common Mistakes

- Writing vague, generic notes that don't reflect what actually happened
- Omitting unresolved objections or difficult moments to make a call look more successful than it was
- Delaying note-writing until details have faded from memory
- Using a note as a substitute for actually escalating a serious concern

## Escalation Guidance

If a note reveals a serious concern — a genuine red flag, a compliance question, or something outside an advisor's authority — that concern should be actively escalated, not just recorded and left in the note for someone to eventually find. See [Internal Escalation Guidelines](/docs/sales-operations/internal-escalation-guidelines) for how to raise it directly.

A candidate note is only useful if it's accurate, specific, and honest — including the parts of a call that didn't go as planned. Write promptly, be specific, and remember that a note documents a situation; it doesn't replace actively escalating a serious concern.
