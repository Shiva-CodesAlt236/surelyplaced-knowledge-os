# Objection Handling Framework

## Purpose

Objections show up in discovery, discussion, and closing calls, and they're often treated as obstacles to get past. This article sets a different starting point: an objection is a candidate telling you, directly, what information they're still missing before they can decide. This framework is the foundation every other article in this module builds on — the specific objections that follow (price, timing, family approval, competing programs, trust, the absence of a guarantee) are all applications of the same underlying approach, not separate techniques.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why an objection is treated as information rather than resistance to overcome
- Apply a consistent four-step approach to any objection, regardless of its specific content
- Distinguish between an objection that's resolvable in the conversation and one that genuinely needs more time or information
- Recognize that handling an objection well can still end in "no," and that this is not a failure
- Avoid the reflex to argue harder when an objection doesn't resolve on the first attempt

## When To Use

- As the foundation for every other article in this module — read this one first
- Any time a candidate raises a concern, hesitation, or pushback at any point in the funnel, not only during a closing call
- When training a newer advisor on how to think about objections before teaching them specific scripts

## Understanding the Candidate's Concern

Underneath almost every objection is one of a small number of real things: missing information, a genuine constraint (time, money, someone else's approval), a bad past experience with a similar program or recruiter, or a deeper doubt about whether this is the right decision that hasn't fully surfaced yet. The specific words a candidate uses rarely tell you which one you're dealing with — that takes a direct follow-up question, not a guess.

It's also worth naming a pattern that shows up across almost every objection type: candidates in this population have often been sold to badly before. That history means an objection is sometimes less about the specific thing being raised and more about testing whether this conversation is going to go the way the last one did. An advisor who responds with genuine curiosity, rather than a rehearsed rebuttal, is usually the first sign to the candidate that this conversation is different.

## Recommended Conversation Framework

**Acknowledge before responding.** Whatever the objection is, the candidate needs to feel heard before they can hear anything back. A quick "that's a fair thing to raise" or "I'm glad you said that directly" costs nothing and keeps the conversation open.

**Ask what's underneath it.** Most objections are shorthand for something more specific. "I need to think about it" might mean a dozen different things. Ask directly rather than responding to the surface version.

**Respond to the real thing, not the first thing.** Once you know what's actually driving the objection, address that — not a generic version of the objection that might not match what this candidate is actually experiencing.

**Accept that resolution isn't the only good outcome.** If, after a genuine conversation, the honest answer is that this isn't the right fit or the right time, that's the framework working correctly, not failing.

## Questions to Ask

1. "Can you tell me more about what's behind that?"
2. "Is this the main thing, or is there something else sitting underneath it too?"
3. "If I could fully resolve this for you right now, would that change where you're at — or is this something that needs time regardless?"
4. "What would need to be true for this not to be a concern anymore?"

## Best Practices

- Treat every objection as legitimate until you understand it, even if it initially sounds like a stalling tactic — most aren't.
- Resist responding to an objection with a longer, more confident version of what you already said. If it didn't land the first time, repeating it rarely helps.
- Keep track of which objections keep resurfacing across a single call — a concern that returns after being "resolved" once usually means the real concern wasn't fully addressed.
- Use the specific objection articles in this module once you know what you're actually dealing with — this framework tells you how to get there.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating every objection as the same generic "objection" and reaching for a one-size-fits-all rebuttal
- Responding to the surface version of a concern without asking what's underneath it
- Arguing harder when an objection doesn't resolve on the first pass, instead of asking a different question
- Treating a resolved-sounding "okay, that makes sense" as real resolution without checking that it actually was
- Continuing to push after a candidate has clearly indicated the honest answer is no, or not yet

## Real Conversation Examples

**Getting past the surface version of an objection:**

> **Candidate:** "I don't know, it just feels like a lot right now."
> **Advisor:** "That's fair — can you tell me more about what's behind that? 'A lot' could mean a few different things."
> **Candidate:** "I guess it's mostly that I'd be adding this on top of a job search I'm already doing, and I'm already stretched."
> **Advisor:** "That's really useful to know — that's a different conversation than if it were about the decision itself. Let's talk about how this would actually fit alongside what you're already doing, rather than assume it just adds to the pile."

**Accepting a genuine "not now" as a real outcome:**

> **Advisor:** "If I could resolve this fully right now, would that change things, or does this need time regardless?"
> **Candidate:** "Honestly, it needs time. I don't think there's anything you could say today that would change that."
> **Advisor:** "I appreciate you being that direct — that's actually really helpful. Let's talk about what a fair amount of time looks like, rather than me trying to talk you out of needing it."

## Key Takeaways

Every objection in this module is an application of the same underlying approach: acknowledge it, find out what's actually underneath it, respond to the real thing, and accept that a genuine, well-handled "no" is a legitimate outcome. The specific objections that follow in this module tell you what to listen for and how to respond to each one — this framework tells you how to think about all of them.
