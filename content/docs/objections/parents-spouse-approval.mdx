# Parents & Spouse Approval

For a lot of candidates, this isn't a decision they make alone — and that's not indecisiveness, it's how the decision is actually structured for them.

**Why this matters:** treating a named decision-maker as a stalling tactic instead of a real factor can push a candidate to decide before they're genuinely ready, or lose their trust entirely.
**When to use this:** any time a candidate names a spouse, parent, or family member as part of how this decision actually gets made.
**What skill this builds:** treating a missing decision-maker as legitimate, and offering concrete support — like a joint call — rather than a vague offer.
**What happens next:** the candidate returns with an actual answer once that conversation has genuinely happened, on the specific timeline you both agreed to.

## Lesson Objective

- Treat a missing decision-maker as a legitimate factor, never an obstacle
- Offer concrete support for the conversation the candidate needs to have
- Set a specific follow-up tied to when that conversation will realistically happen

## Coaching Notes

<CoachingNotes title="Coaching Notes">
Offer something concrete — a summary, an invitation to join a call — rather than just waiting passively for the candidate to come back on their own.
</CoachingNotes>

<CoachingNotes title="Why This Worked">
Asking what concern his parents would likely raise meant the summary could actually pre-answer it, instead of being generic. That's the difference between real support and a token gesture.
</CoachingNotes>
