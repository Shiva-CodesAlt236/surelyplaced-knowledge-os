# Parents & Spouse Approval

## Purpose

Many candidates aren't the sole decision-maker in their own career decisions — a spouse, a parent, or a family member is often part of the conversation, even when they're not on the call. This is not a stalling tactic to route around, and treating it as one is one of the fastest ways to lose a candidate's trust. This article covers how to recognize this factor, respect it, and support the candidate in having the conversation they actually need to have, rather than trying to get a decision that skips past it.

## Learning Objectives

- Recognize when a family decision-maker is genuinely part of the process versus when it's being used to avoid a direct answer
- Respond to this factor with respect rather than treating it as an obstacle
- Offer concrete, honest ways to support a candidate's conversation with a spouse or family member
- Avoid any language that pressures a candidate to decide without the person they've said they need to consult
- Recognize the cultural and financial weight this decision can carry for candidates who've relocated or are supporting family

## When To Use

- Any time a candidate mentions needing to talk to a spouse, parent, or family member before deciding
- When a candidate's hesitation seems tied to someone else's opinion or approval, even if they haven't said so directly
- When a candidate who seemed ready suddenly becomes less certain, and family involvement hasn't yet been asked about directly

## Understanding the Candidate's Concern

For a meaningful number of candidates in this population — particularly those who've relocated for work or education, or who are supporting family financially — a decision like this genuinely isn't theirs alone to make quickly. A spouse may share the financial risk directly. A parent may have been part of the original decision to move abroad and reasonably expects to be part of what happens next. This isn't indecisiveness; it's how the decision is actually structured for many candidates, and it deserves to be treated as a real fact about their situation, not a soft excuse.

At the same time, it's honest to acknowledge that "I need to talk to my spouse" can occasionally be used as a way to end a conversation without saying no directly. The way to tell the difference isn't to guess — it's to ask directly and pay attention to how specifically the candidate answers.

## Recommended Conversation Framework

Acknowledge the need to involve someone else as legitimate, immediately and without hesitation — this is not a moment to push back or express surprise.

Ask what would actually help that conversation go well — information, a written summary, an invitation for the other person to join a future call. This shifts the advisor's role from managing an objection to genuinely supporting a real conversation the candidate needs to have.

Offer to include the other person directly in a future call if the candidate is open to it, without pressuring them to do so if they'd rather have that conversation privately.

Set a specific follow-up tied to when that conversation will realistically happen, rather than a vague "let me know."

## Questions to Ask

1. "That makes complete sense — is there anything that would help that conversation go well, like a summary of what we've covered?"
2. "Would it be useful to have them join a future call, or is this a conversation you'd rather have on your own first?"
3. "Is there a specific concern you expect they'll raise, so we can make sure you have a clear answer for it?"
4. "When do you think you'll have a chance to talk with them, so I know when it makes sense to follow up?"

## Best Practices

- Treat this factor as a normal, common part of the decision — never as a red flag or a delay tactic by default.
- Offer concrete support (a summary, an invitation to join a call) rather than just waiting passively for the candidate to come back.
- Ask what concern the candidate expects to come up, so they're not walking into that conversation unprepared.
- Respect a candidate's choice to have that conversation privately, without pushing for a joint call if they haven't asked for one.
- Set a real follow-up date tied to when the conversation will actually happen, not an arbitrary standard interval.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating "I need to talk to my spouse" as synonymous with "no" or as a stalling tactic by default
- Pushing for a same-call decision after a candidate has clearly named a missing decision-maker
- Failing to offer any concrete help for the conversation the candidate is about to have
- Assuming every candidate wants the other person on a call, rather than asking
- Following up too soon, before the candidate has realistically had the chance to have that conversation

## Real Conversation Examples

**Offering concrete support without pressure:**

> **Candidate:** "I'd want to talk this over with my spouse before deciding anything."
> **Advisor:** "That makes complete sense, and I'd actually be surprised if you didn't want to. Is there anything that would help that conversation — a written summary of what we've covered, or would it be useful to have them join a future call?"
> **Candidate:** "A summary would actually help a lot, honestly."
> **Advisor:** "I'll get that to you. Is there a specific concern you expect they'll raise, so we make sure that's addressed clearly in what I send?"

**Distinguishing a real factor from a polite exit:**

> **Advisor:** "Is there a specific concern you expect your parents will raise?"
> **Candidate:** "Not really, I just... yeah, I don't know."
> **Advisor:** "That's okay too — you don't need to have it all mapped out. When do you think you'll have a chance to talk with them, so I know when to check back in?"
> **Candidate:** "Probably this weekend."
> **Advisor:** "Good, I'll follow up early next week then."

## Key Takeaways

Needing to involve a spouse, parent, or family member in a decision like this is common and legitimate, not a stalling tactic to route around. Acknowledge it immediately, offer concrete support for the conversation the candidate needs to have, and set a real follow-up tied to when that conversation will actually happen. Never pressure a candidate to decide without the person they've said they need to consult.
