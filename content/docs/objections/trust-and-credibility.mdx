# Trust and Credibility

## Purpose

Direct skepticism — "how do I know this is legitimate," "is this basically a scam," or a more guarded version of the same concern — comes up often enough in this space to deserve its own article. This is not an objection to be defensive about. It's a reasonable response from candidates who've likely encountered recruiters, visa "consultants," or programs that overpromised before. This article covers how to meet that skepticism directly and honestly, without over-explaining or sounding defensive.

## Learning Objectives

- Respond to direct skepticism without becoming defensive or over-explaining
- Recognize that specificity, not reassurance, is what actually earns credibility in this conversation
- Ask what's behind a candidate's skepticism rather than assuming you already know
- Avoid any language that sounds like it's trying too hard to prove legitimacy
- Know what this article cannot resolve, and where to route a candidate's specific verification questions

## When To Use

- When a candidate directly asks whether Surely Placed is legitimate, or expresses skepticism in less direct terms
- When a candidate mentions a prior bad experience with a recruiter, program, or consultancy that's clearly shaping their guard
- Early in discovery, when general wariness is present even without being stated outright

## Understanding the Candidate's Concern

Skepticism in this space is earned, not irrational. Many candidates have already been on the receiving end of vague promises, disappearing recruiters, or programs that oversold outcomes. A candidate asking a pointed question about legitimacy isn't being difficult — they're doing exactly what a careful person should do before committing time, money, and trust to something that affects their career and, often, their ability to stay in a country they've worked hard to build a life in.

It's worth noting that over-reassuring in response to this kind of question often backfires. A candidate who's already skeptical will notice if an advisor responds with more enthusiasm than substance, and it tends to confirm the doubt rather than resolve it. What actually works is the opposite: calm, specific, unhurried answers.

## Recommended Conversation Framework

Don't get defensive, and don't over-explain. A skeptical question deserves a direct, calm answer, not a flood of reassurance.

Ask what's behind the skepticism specifically — a bad past experience, something someone told them, a general caution about this space. This tells you what to actually address, rather than guessing.

Answer with specificity rather than enthusiasm. If a candidate asks what makes Surely Placed different, answer with concrete, checkable information rather than confident-sounding generalities.

Be honest about the limits of what a single phone conversation can prove. Trust is built over the course of the relationship, not resolved in one answer — say so if it's true, rather than implying this one conversation should fully settle it.

## Questions to Ask

1. "Can I ask what's behind that — has something happened before that's making you cautious here?"
2. "What would actually help you feel confident this is legitimate, beyond just me telling you it is?"
3. "Is there something specific you'd want to verify or check before going further?"
4. "What's made other programs or recruiters feel untrustworthy to you in the past, so I know what to avoid repeating?"

## Best Practices

- Respond calmly and specifically rather than with more enthusiasm or reassurance.
- Ask what's behind the skepticism rather than assuming you already know the source of it.
- Be honest that one conversation can't fully resolve trust — trust builds over the relationship, not in a single answer.
- Never respond to a skepticism question with pressure to decide faster, which only reinforces the candidate's caution.

## Common Mistakes

- Responding to skepticism with more enthusiasm or reassurance, which tends to deepen doubt rather than resolve it
- Getting defensive, which can read as confirming there's something to be defensive about
- Failing to ask what's actually behind the skepticism before responding
- Trying to fully resolve trust in a single answer instead of acknowledging that it builds over time
- Rushing the candidate past their own caution instead of respecting it

## Real Conversation Examples

**Meeting direct skepticism calmly:**

> **Candidate:** "Honestly, how do I know this isn't just another program that's going to overpromise?"
> **Advisor:** "That's a completely fair question, and I'd rather you ask it directly than just wonder. Can I ask — has something like that happened to you before?"
> **Candidate:** "Yeah, actually, I paid an application fee to a recruiter last year and never heard from them again."
> **Advisor:** "That's a genuinely bad experience, and I understand why it's shaping how you're approaching this conversation. I'm not going to try to talk you out of that caution — I'd rather you keep it and just judge this conversation on its own specifics as we go."

**Being honest about the limits of a single conversation:**

> **Candidate:** "I guess I just want to feel sure before committing to anything."
> **Advisor:** "That makes sense, and I want to be honest with you — I don't think one phone call is really enough to make you fully sure, and I wouldn't trust a program that claimed it could. What I can do is answer anything specific and be straightforward with you as we go, and let that build over time rather than ask you to just take my word for it today."
