# Need Time to Think

## Purpose

"I need some time to think about it" is the most common objection an advisor will hear, and it can show up almost anywhere — during discovery, midway through a discussion call, or right after a closing ask. This article covers the objection itself: what it usually means, how to tell its different versions apart, and how to respond honestly wherever it comes up. For the specific version of this moment that happens right after a direct closing ask, see [Handling Final Hesitation](/docs/closing/handling-final-hesitation), which covers that particular conversational beat in more detail.

## Learning Objectives

- Recognize that "I need time to think" said early in the funnel and said at the closing stage usually mean different things
- Ask a direct, non-pressuring follow-up that surfaces what the candidate actually needs time for
- Tell the difference between a genuine need for reflection and a polite way of disengaging from the conversation
- Avoid treating every instance of this phrase as a problem to solve on the spot
- Set a real, specific follow-up when time is genuinely what's needed

## When To Use

- Any time a candidate says a version of "I need to think about it," at any stage of the funnel
- When a candidate's tone shifts to noncommittal mid-conversation, even before anything has been directly asked
- When following up with a candidate who previously asked for time, to check in without pressuring

## Understanding the Candidate's Concern

This phrase means different things depending on where it shows up. Early in discovery, it often means the candidate hasn't yet decided whether this conversation is worth continuing — it's a soft exit, not necessarily a rejection. Midway through a discussion call, it can mean something specific just landed that the candidate wasn't expecting and genuinely needs to process. At the closing stage, it can mean any of several things: real deliberation, a missing decision-maker, or a polite way of saying no without having to say it directly.

It's worth being honest that not every instance of this phrase hides something deeper. Some candidates are simply careful decision-makers who take real time on decisions of this size, and treating that instinct as a puzzle to be solved can itself feel like pressure. The skill here is telling the difference, not assuming one explanation fits every case.

## Recommended Conversation Framework

Acknowledge the request for time without treating it as a setback — "of course" or "that makes sense" keeps the conversation open rather than defensive.

Ask directly what they'd actually be thinking through. This single question does more work than anything else in this article: it either surfaces a specific, addressable concern, or it confirms that this is a genuine need for reflection rather than something resolvable today.

If something specific surfaces, address it directly and completely, then check again whether that resolves things — don't assume it does.

If nothing specific surfaces after asking directly, more than once, gently, respect that as real. Set a specific follow-up rather than leaving it open-ended, and end the conversation warmly.

## Questions to Ask

1. "Of course — can I ask what specifically you'd want to think through?"
2. "Is there something that came up in this conversation that you weren't expecting, or is this more of a general 'let me sit with it' kind of thing?"
3. "If I could address whatever's on your mind right now, would that change things today, or does this need time regardless?"
4. "What would be a fair amount of time, so I know when it makes sense to check back in?"

## Best Practices

- Ask "what specifically" rather than "why" — it invites a concrete answer rather than putting the candidate on the defensive.
- Don't keep probing once a candidate has clearly indicated, more than once, that they just need time — repeated probing past that point starts to feel like pressure.
- Match the level of follow-up to where in the funnel this came up — an early discovery "let me think about it" usually needs a lighter touch than one after a closing ask.
- Always set a specific date for following up, never an open-ended "reach out whenever."

## Common Mistakes

- Treating every instance of this phrase as a closing-stage objection, even when it shows up much earlier in the funnel
- Responding with a longer, more urgent pitch instead of asking what's actually behind the request for time
- Assuming the request for time is a soft "no" without checking, or assuming it's genuine reflection without checking either
- Setting a vague follow-up instead of a specific one
- Continuing to ask "what's really going on" repeatedly after a candidate has clearly said they just need time

## Real Conversation Examples

**Early in discovery, a soft disengagement:**

> **Candidate:** "This all sounds fine, I think I just need to think about it for now."
> **Advisor:** "Totally fair, we've barely gotten into the specifics yet. Can I ask — is there something already sitting with you, or does it just feel early to commit to anything?"
> **Candidate:** "Honestly, it feels early. I wanted to see what this was before deciding if it's worth more of my time."
> **Advisor:** "That makes complete sense. No pressure at all — if it's helpful, I can share a bit more about what a next conversation would actually cover, so you have more to go on when you do decide."

**Midway through a discussion call, something specific landed:**

> **Candidate:** "I need a minute — I wasn't expecting the process to involve that much on my end."
> **Advisor:** "That's a fair reaction, and I'd rather you say that than just nod along. What specifically caught you off guard about it?"
> **Candidate:** "I guess I assumed more of it would be done for me."
> **Advisor:** "Good to know, and worth addressing directly rather than moving past it. Let's talk about that specifically before we go any further."

## Key Takeaways

"I need time to think" means different things depending on when it shows up, and the only reliable way to know which version you're facing is to ask directly. Address a specific concern if one surfaces, respect a genuine need for time without continuing to probe, and always set a real follow-up date rather than leaving things open-ended.
