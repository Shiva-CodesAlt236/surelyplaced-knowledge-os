# Objection Handling Checklist

## Purpose

This article closes out the Objection Handling module the same way the checklists in the Discovery, Discussion, and Closing modules close out theirs — a practical check to run after any objection, on any call, to confirm it was actually handled well rather than just gotten past.

The checklist itself:

1. Did I acknowledge the objection before responding to it?
2. Did I ask what was actually behind it, rather than responding to the surface version?
3. Did I respond to the real concern, once I understood it — not a generic version of the objection?
4. Was every figure or claim I made something I actually knew to be current, rather than something that just sounded right?
5. If the objection touched a genuine policy gap (like simultaneous engagement with another provider), did I say so honestly instead of guessing?
6. If the objection didn't resolve, did I accept that as a legitimate outcome instead of pushing harder?
7. If a follow-up was needed, did I set something specific rather than open-ended?
8. Did I avoid criticizing a competitor, named or unnamed, at any point?

If any answer isn't a clear yes, that's useful information about what to revisit — not a sign the call went badly.

## Learning Objectives

- Use this checklist as a genuine check after any objection, not only at the end of a call
- Confirm that the underlying objection-handling framework was actually followed, not just a specific script
- Catch moments where an unconfirmed claim, guess, or competitor criticism may have slipped into a response
- Recognize when an unresolved objection was handled honestly versus when it was simply dropped
- Build the habit of running this check consistently enough that it becomes automatic

## When To Use

- After any objection is raised and addressed, at any point in a call
- At the end of a call that included one or more objections, as a final review before moving on
- When reviewing a recorded or logged call afterward, either individually or with a manager

## Understanding the Candidate's Concern

This article doesn't introduce a new concern to understand — it exists to confirm that whatever concern actually came up during the call was engaged with honestly. The risk this checklist guards against isn't a candidate's concern; it's an advisor's own habits, like reaching for a familiar response before genuinely understanding what was being asked, or letting an unresolved objection quietly slide by without being addressed on its own terms.

## Recommended Conversation Framework

Run the first three checklist items in real time, during the objection itself — acknowledge, ask what's underneath, respond to the real thing. These aren't meant to be checked only in hindsight.

Run items four through eight as a discipline check, either in the moment or immediately after — did anything get stated that shouldn't have, and was the outcome, whatever it was, handled honestly.

If a gap turns up on review, decide whether it needs to be corrected with the candidate directly (for example, following up to clarify something that was stated with more confidence than was warranted) or simply noted for next time.

## Questions to Ask

1. "Did I actually understand what was behind that objection, or did I respond to my best guess?"
2. "Is there anything I said just now that I'm not fully certain was accurate?"
3. "Did that objection actually get resolved, or did it just stop being mentioned?"
4. "If I'm following up, do I have something specific and real to follow up on?"

## Best Practices

- Treat this checklist as a running habit during the call, not just a post-call review.
- Be honest with yourself about whether an objection was resolved or just quietly dropped — those are very different outcomes.
- If you're ever unsure whether something you said was accurate, flag it and confirm afterward rather than letting it go unchecked.
- Use the specific objection articles in this module as your reference for what "handled well" looks like for a given objection type.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating an objection that stopped being mentioned as the same thing as an objection that was actually resolved
- Skipping the self-check on accuracy, especially under the pressure of a live call
- Letting a competitor comparison drift into criticism without noticing it happened
- Ending a call with a vague sense that objections were "handled" without being able to say specifically how
- Using this checklist only in review, rather than as a habit during the conversation itself

## Real Conversation Examples

**Catching an objection that was dropped, not resolved:**

> *(Advisor's post-call note, included as a teaching example rather than a live script)*
> "Looking back, the candidate raised a price concern about twenty minutes in, and I moved on to the next topic pretty quickly. I don't think it was actually resolved — I think it just stopped coming up. I'm going to follow up specifically on that rather than assume it's settled."

**Self-correcting an unconfirmed claim after the call:**

> *(Advisor's post-call note)*
> "I mentioned a rough timeline during the call that I wasn't actually sure was current. I'm going to reach out and confirm the accurate version rather than let that stand."

## Key Takeaways

Handling an objection well means acknowledging it, understanding what's actually behind it, and responding honestly to the real concern — and this checklist exists to confirm that actually happened, rather than just assuming it did because the conversation moved on. Be honest with yourself about whether an objection was resolved or just dropped, and always flag anything you weren't fully certain was accurate rather than letting it stand unchecked.
