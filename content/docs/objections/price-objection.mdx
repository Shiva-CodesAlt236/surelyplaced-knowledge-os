# Price Objection

## Purpose

A price objection can show up well before the formal investment conversation — sometimes as early as discovery, when a candidate asks "so how much does this cost?" before they understand what it includes. This article covers how to respond to a price objection wherever it shows up in the funnel. It is a companion to [Discussing Investment](/docs/discussion/discussing-investment), which covers the structured, planned cost conversation later in a discussion call; this article is about handling cost-related pushback when it arrives earlier, or in a sharper form, than that planned conversation.

## Learning Objectives

- Respond to an early or unplanned price question without either dodging it or answering with an unconfirmed figure
- Distinguish between a price objection about the number itself and one about affordability or timing more broadly
- Reconnect a price objection to value that's already been established, or acknowledge honestly when it hasn't been yet
- Avoid discounting, promising special arrangements, or inventing flexible terms to make an objection disappear
- Recognize when a price objection is really a confidence or trust objection wearing a cost costume

## When To Use

- When a candidate raises cost before the recommendation or process has been explained (see [Structuring the Discussion Call](/docs/discussion/structuring-the-discussion-call))
- When a candidate pushes back on cost after hearing it, beyond the initial reaction covered in [Discussing Investment](/docs/discussion/discussing-investment)
- When a candidate compares Surely Placed's cost to a cheaper alternative or to doing the job search alone

## Understanding the Candidate's Concern

A price objection raised early in the funnel is often not really about the number — it's a candidate trying to figure out whether this conversation is worth their continued time before they've invested more of it. A price objection raised later, after the value has actually been explained, is a different thing: it's a real evaluation of whether what's being offered is worth what it costs, and it deserves to be treated as a real evaluation, not brushed aside.

It's worth remembering that for a meaningful share of candidates in this population, cost isn't just a number — it intersects with real financial pressure tied to unemployment, a visa timeline, or supporting family. A price objection from a candidate under that kind of pressure is not the same conversation as one from a candidate who's simply comparison-shopping, even if the words are similar.

## Recommended Conversation Framework

If cost comes up before value has been established, don't answer it in a vacuum. Acknowledge the question honestly, and ask permission to cover what's included first, so the number has something to be evaluated against.

If cost comes up after value has been discussed, connect the objection directly back to what was covered — remind the candidate concretely what the cost is attached to, rather than repeating the number more insistently.

Ask what's actually behind the objection: the number itself, the timing, or a broader affordability concern. Each of those is a different conversation, and responding to the wrong one wastes the candidate's patience.

Never offer a discount, waiver, or nonstandard arrangement to resolve the objection. If a candidate asks directly, route the request to the correct current internal process rather than answering from assumption.

## Questions to Ask

1. "Can I ask what's behind that — is it the number itself, or is it more about timing or affordability more broadly?"
2. "Compared to what you were expecting, is this higher, lower, or about what you thought?"
3. "Setting the number aside for a second — does what's included actually make sense for what you're trying to get out of this?"
4. "Is there a specific number or range you had in mind, so I understand what we're comparing against?"

## Best Practices

- Never state a specific price, discount, or payment term from memory — confirm the current, accurate figure through the proper resource, every time.
- Reconnect the cost to value already discussed rather than treating the objection as purely about the number.
- If value hasn't actually been established yet, say so honestly and go back to it rather than defending a number in isolation.
- Keep your tone steady rather than defensive — a price objection is a normal part of an honest sales conversation, not a crisis to manage.

## Common Mistakes

- Answering an early price question with a specific figure before any context has been established
- Responding to hesitation by repeating the value pitch louder instead of asking what's actually behind the objection
- Offering an unauthorized discount or special arrangement to make the objection go away in the moment
- Treating every price objection as identical, when the number, the timing, and the affordability concern each require a different response
- Getting defensive, which tends to make a reasonable objection feel like an accusation was made

## Real Conversation Examples

**An early, unplanned price question:**

> **Candidate:** "Before we go any further, what does this actually cost?"
> **Advisor:** "Fair question, and I'll get you a clear, accurate answer — I don't want to give you a number without the context of what it's actually attached to, since that's the part that makes the number make sense. Can we cover what's included first, and then get straight to cost?"
> **Candidate:** "Sure, that's fine."

**A price objection after value has been discussed:**

> **Candidate:** "I get what's included, but it's still more than I was expecting."
> **Advisor:** "That's a fair reaction, and I'd rather you say that directly than just go quiet. Can I ask — is it the number itself that feels off, or is it more a question of whether this is the right time for you financially?"
> **Candidate:** "Probably more the second thing, honestly."
> **Advisor:** "That's a different conversation than negotiating the number, and it's worth being honest about. Let's talk about that directly."

## Key Takeaways

A price objection deserves a real answer, not a reflex. Never state a figure that hasn't been confirmed as current, connect cost back to established value rather than defending a number on its own, and find out honestly whether the objection is about the number, the timing, or something broader. Never discount or improvise terms to make the objection disappear — route those requests to the correct process instead.
