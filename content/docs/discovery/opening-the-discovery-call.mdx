# Opening the Discovery Call

## Purpose

The first few minutes of a discovery call decide how honest the rest of it is going to be. This article is about that specific window — not general trust-building, which is covered elsewhere, but the concrete job of opening *this particular call type* well: setting a clear agenda, telling the candidate what to expect, and making it obvious from the first exchange that you're here to figure out if this is a fit, not to talk them into anything.

Surely Placed doesn't sell jobs and doesn't guarantee placement. That has to be true in how the call opens, not just in the fine print somewhere later. A candidate who hears a confident, vague pitch in the first minute will spend the rest of the call waiting for the catch. A candidate who hears "I'm going to ask you some questions so we can figure out together whether this makes sense" relaxes and actually answers honestly.

## Learning Objectives

- State the purpose of a discovery call to a candidate in one or two plain sentences
- Set an agenda that gives the candidate a sense of control over the conversation
- Ask a grounding question that surfaces why the candidate reached out, before anything else
- Recognize when an opening has gone sideways and reset it without making it awkward
- Avoid the handful of opening habits that quietly put candidates on guard

## Candidate Psychology

Most candidates dialing into a discovery call have done this before — with a recruiter, a bootcamp, an agency — and it didn't go anywhere good. So they're not arriving blank. They're arriving with a prediction already loaded: *this is going to be a pitch, and I'm going to have to sit through it before I get to ask my real questions.*

That prediction shapes how they behave in the first minute whether they realize it or not. Some candidates go quiet and short with their answers, testing to see if the advisor notices or just keeps talking. Others front-load a defensive question early — "so what's this actually going to cost me" — before the advisor has even explained what the call is for, because they want to get the uncomfortable part out of the way on their terms.

A smaller number of candidates come in the opposite direction: eager, talking fast, hoping this is finally the call that solves the problem they've been stuck on. That's its own risk. An advisor who doesn't slow that energy down a little will end up rushing straight past real discovery and into a recommendation nobody's actually earned yet.

## Advisor Objective

Tell the candidate plainly what this call is and isn't, get them talking about their actual situation within the first couple of minutes, and set expectations that this is a two-way conversation about fit — not a presentation.

## When To Use

- At the start of every discovery call, without exception, even with a candidate who seems to already know what Surely Placed does
- When a candidate joins the call sounding rushed, guarded, or flat, and the opening needs to work a little harder than usual
- When picking a discovery conversation back up after it stalled or got rescheduled, and trust needs a light reset before continuing

## Discovery Questions

1. "Before we get into anything, I want to tell you how I'd like to run this call — is that okay?"
2. "What made you reach out to us specifically, as opposed to just continuing your job search on your own?"
3. "Have you talked to anyone else — another program, a recruiter — about this kind of move?"
4. "What would make this call worth your time, even if we end up deciding this isn't the right fit?"

## Why Each Question Matters

The first question isn't really a question — it's permission-asking, and it matters because it hands the candidate a small amount of control right away. Most candidates say yes immediately, but the act of asking changes the tone of everything that follows.

The second question gets at the actual trigger behind the call. "I want a better job" is not the same as "I got laid off three weeks ago" or "my manager told me there's no room to grow here." The real trigger tells you what's actually at stake for this person and how much patience they have for a slow, careful process versus how much urgency you're working with.

The third question does two things at once. It tells you what comparisons the candidate is already making in their head, and it gives you a natural opening to acknowledge, out loud, that you know this space has plenty of people who overpromise. Naming that early is worth more than almost anything else you can say in the first few minutes.

The fourth question reframes the whole call as being run for the candidate's benefit, not yours. It also gives you something concrete to check back against near the end of the call — did you actually deliver on what they said they needed?

## How to Interpret Candidate Answers

If a candidate answers the second question with something specific and time-stamped — a layoff, a deadline, a bad performance review — take that seriously and let it set the pace. Don't rush past a real trigger to get through your opening script.

If the answer is vague ("just exploring options"), don't push on it immediately. Vague answers at the start are common and not usually evasive — the real motivation often surfaces a few questions later, once the candidate warms up. Note it and move on.

If a candidate answers the third question with a clearly negative experience, that's a signal to slow down, not speed up. They're telling you what they're testing you against. If they answer with "no, you're the first," that usually means less built-up defensiveness, but also less context for why they should trust you yet — you're starting from scratch either way, just for different reasons.

Watch tone as much as content on the fourth question. A candidate who answers thoughtfully is signaling they're taking the call seriously. A candidate who brushes it off with "I don't know, just tell me what you've got" is often either in a hurry or testing whether you'll respect the boundary they just tried to set by not answering — either way, worth noting and adjusting pace for.

## Follow-up Questions

- If the trigger sounds urgent: "How much of a factor is timing for you right now — are we talking weeks, or is there more room than that?"
- If the candidate mentions a prior bad experience: "What specifically went wrong there, so I know what to do differently?"
- If the answer to the fourth question is vague: "Even one thing you'd want to walk away knowing — what would that be?"
- If the candidate seems rushed: "Is now still a good time, or would it be better to find twenty minutes later this week when you can actually focus?"

## Common Mistakes

- Diving into a description of the program before asking a single question about the candidate
- Treating the opening as a script to get through rather than an actual exchange to pay attention to
- Skipping the "what made you reach out" question and assuming you already know, based on how the candidate found you
- Filling every pause in the first minute instead of letting the candidate finish a thought at their own pace
- Opening the exact same way regardless of whether the candidate sounds rushed, guarded, or eager — the opening should flex to what you're hearing

## Things Never To Say

- "This will only take a few minutes." (If it's genuinely a real discovery conversation, it usually takes longer than that — don't set a false expectation just to get someone on the line.)
- "I already have a good sense of what you need." (Said in the first minute, before real discovery has happened, this just sounds like you're not actually going to listen.)
- Anything that implies the call itself guarantees an outcome — "let's get you placed" type language has no place in an opening, or anywhere else. We diagnose before we recommend, and that's true from word one.

## Conversation Example

> **Advisor:** "Before we get into anything, I want to tell you how I'd like to run this call, if that's alright."
> **Candidate:** "Sure, go ahead."
> **Advisor:** "Great — so my job today isn't to convince you of anything. It's to actually understand your situation well enough that by the end of this call, we both know whether Surely Placed makes sense for you or not. If it doesn't, I'll tell you that directly. Sound fair?"
> **Candidate:** "Yeah, that's... actually refreshing, honestly."
> **Advisor:** "Good to hear. So let's start there — what made you reach out to us specifically, instead of just continuing to job search on your own?"
> **Candidate:** "I've been applying for months and getting almost nothing back. I saw a post about your program and figured it was worth a shot, but I'll be honest, I've talked to a couple of other places already and it didn't go anywhere."
> **Advisor:** "Appreciate you telling me that upfront. What happened with those other conversations — what didn't work about them?"
> **Candidate:** "They just kept pushing me to sign up without really asking me anything about my background first."
> **Advisor:** "Good to know, and I'll do the opposite of that today. Let's actually get into your background before I say anything about what we do."

## Advanced Advisor Tips

Once the mechanics of an opening are automatic, the real skill is calibration — reading, in the first fifteen seconds of tone and pacing, whether this candidate needs you to slow down or whether they'll actually get frustrated if you spend too long on setup. Some candidates want the permission-asking and framing; others just want to get to their questions. Both are fine — the goal isn't to run the same opening every time, it's to run an opening that fits the person on the other end of the line.

It's also worth practicing how you name a prior bad experience without sounding like you're distancing yourself too hard from "other" recruiters or programs in a way that starts to sound defensive itself. The strongest version of this is matter-of-fact, not apologetic on behalf of an entire industry.

## Summary

A discovery call opens well when the candidate understands, within the first minute, what this conversation is for and what it isn't. Ask permission to set the agenda, find out what actually brought them to this call, ask directly about prior experiences so you know what you're working against, and give them a reason to invest in the conversation beyond just waiting to hear a pitch. Everything that follows in discovery is easier when the opening did its job.
