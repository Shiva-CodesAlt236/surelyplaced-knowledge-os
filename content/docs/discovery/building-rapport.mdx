# Building Rapport

## Purpose

Rapport isn't small talk, and it isn't a personality trait some advisors happen to have and others don't. It's a set of behaviors that make a candidate feel like they're actually being listened to, and it has to hold up for the full length of a discovery call, not just the opening minute. This article is about how to build and keep rapport once the call is already moving — through questions, through how you respond to answers, and through the small moments where a candidate is quietly deciding whether to keep being honest with you.

## Learning Objectives

- Tell the difference between rapport that's actually being built and rapport that's just being performed
- Respond to a candidate's answer in a way that proves you were listening, not just waiting to ask the next question
- Recognize the moments mid-call where rapport is most at risk of slipping
- Use silence deliberately instead of filling it out of your own discomfort
- Recover rapport with a candidate who's gone quiet or guarded partway through the call

## Candidate Psychology

Rapport breaks down in a specific, predictable way on these calls: a candidate starts out reasonably open, then somewhere in the middle of discovery they say something a little vulnerable — an employment gap, a visa worry, a past rejection — and they watch closely to see how the advisor handles it. If the advisor rushes past it, or worse, pivots straight back to a scripted question, the candidate quietly recalibrates and starts giving shorter, safer answers for the rest of the call. You usually can't tell this happened in the moment. You just notice, later, that the last fifteen minutes gave you a lot less to work with than the first fifteen.

There's also a subtler version of this with candidates who are naturally more guarded to begin with — often candidates who've been burned before, or who come from a professional culture where showing uncertainty doesn't come easily. With this group, rapport isn't built through warmth alone. It's built through demonstrated competence — the advisor asking a question specific enough that the candidate thinks, *okay, this person actually knows what they're talking about* — and warmth follows from that, not the other way around.

## Advisor Objective

Keep the candidate talking honestly for the entire length of the call by responding to what they actually say — not what you expected them to say — and by treating small moments of vulnerability as things to handle carefully, not things to move past quickly.

## When To Use

- Throughout the entire discovery call, not just at the start — rapport needs maintenance, not a one-time setup
- Immediately after a candidate shares something that sounds even slightly vulnerable or uncertain
- When you notice a candidate's answers getting shorter or more guarded as the call goes on
- When resuming a call after any kind of interruption or awkward moment

## Discovery Questions

1. "Can you say more about that?" (used deliberately, after any answer that felt like it had more underneath it)
2. "What was that actually like for you?" (used after a candidate mentions something factual — a layoff, a move, a career change — without unpacking the experience of it)
3. "Is that something you're comfortable talking through, or would you rather I just note it and move on?"
4. "What haven't I asked you yet that you expected me to?"

## Why Each Question Matters

"Can you say more about that" is the single most useful sentence in this entire skill. It costs nothing to ask, it works almost anywhere in a conversation, and it directly tells the candidate their answer mattered enough to go deeper. Advisors who use this consistently get noticeably richer discovery than advisors who move straight from one scripted question to the next.

Asking what something was "actually like" turns a fact into a story, and stories are where real information lives. A candidate who says "I got laid off in March" has told you an event. A candidate who then tells you what that was actually like has told you what they're afraid of, what they're proud of surviving, and often what they're looking for now, whether they realize it or not.

The third question matters because not every candidate wants to go deeper on every topic, and forcing it is its own kind of rapport damage. Asking for permission before pushing on something sensitive signals respect, and most candidates will actually choose to keep talking once they've been given the choice not to.

The fourth question is a rapport repair tool as much as a discovery one. It hands the floor back to the candidate directly and often surfaces something the advisor genuinely missed.

## How to Interpret Candidate Answers

A candidate who responds to "can you say more about that" with real elaboration is telling you rapport is intact — keep going at this depth. A candidate who gives you a slightly longer but still guarded answer is telling you they're willing but not fully relaxed yet; that's a cue to keep your own tone warm and unhurried rather than pushing further immediately.

If a candidate declines the invitation in question three ("I'd rather just move on"), respect that completely and don't circle back to it later in the same call unless they bring it up again themselves. Pushing past a stated boundary is one of the fastest ways to lose a candidate's trust for the rest of the conversation.

If the answer to "what haven't I asked you" surfaces something significant, that's often the single most important piece of information in the whole call — treat it that way, not as an aside to note and return to your list.

## Follow-up Questions

- After a candidate opens up about something difficult: "Thank you for telling me that — has that shaped how you're thinking about this move?"
- If a candidate seems to be testing the waters with a small disclosure: "That sounds like it mattered a lot — I'm glad you brought it up."
- If rapport seems to be slipping (shorter answers, flatter tone): "I want to check in — is this pace working for you, or would it help to slow down?"
- After the "what haven't I asked" question surfaces something new: "That's really useful — can we spend a few minutes on that before we go further?"

## Common Mistakes

- Responding to a vulnerable disclosure by immediately moving to the next scripted question instead of acknowledging what was just said
- Treating rapport as something established once at the start of the call rather than something maintained throughout
- Filling every silence immediately, which trains candidates to stop pausing to think and start giving quicker, shallower answers
- Using "can you say more about that" so mechanically that it starts to sound like a script rather than genuine curiosity
- Pushing a candidate to elaborate on something after they've clearly signaled they'd rather not

## Things Never To Say

- "That's rough, anyway, so let's move on to..." (Acknowledging something painful in the same breath as brushing past it undoes any credit for acknowledging it at all.)
- "I totally understand exactly how you feel." (Nobody can actually know that, and it tends to shut a candidate down rather than open them up — a specific, curious follow-up does more work than a blanket claim of understanding.)
- Any comparison to another candidate's situation ("I had someone last week going through the exact same thing") — it can come across as making the moment about someone else, or breaching another candidate's privacy, and it's rarely necessary.

## Conversation Example

> **Candidate:** "Yeah, I was actually let go in March. It's fine, it happens."
> **Advisor:** "Can you say more about that? I don't want to just note it and move on if there's more to it."
> **Candidate:** "I mean... it wasn't performance-related, they said it was a restructuring thing, but it still felt personal, you know? I'd been there four years."
> **Advisor:** "That makes sense — four years is a long time, restructuring or not, that's going to feel personal no matter what they call it. What was that actually like for you, in the weeks after?"
> **Candidate:** "Honestly, kind of destabilizing. I started applying to anything, not really thinking about what I actually wanted."
> **Advisor:** "That's a really common pattern, and it's useful for me to know — it tells me part of what we should figure out today is what you actually want, separate from what felt safe to apply to in the panic of it. Does that sound right?"
> **Candidate:** "Yeah. Yeah, actually, that's exactly it."

## Advanced Advisor Tips

Experienced advisors learn to notice their own instinct to fill silence and treat it as a signal, not a problem to fix. If you notice the urge to jump in right after a candidate finishes a sentence, that's often the exact moment worth holding back for one more beat — candidates frequently add the most important part of an answer in the few seconds after they thought they were done.

It's also worth developing a light touch with question three — offering candidates an out doesn't mean you're expected to use it defensively or apologetically. Said plainly and warmly, it usually increases how much a candidate is willing to share, not decreases it.

## Summary

Rapport on a discovery call is built and rebuilt continuously, not established once and coasted on. Respond to what candidates actually say, not what you expected to hear. Ask them to go deeper when something sounds like it matters, give them room to decline when it doesn't feel right, and pay attention to the moments where their answers start getting shorter — that's rapport slipping, and it's recoverable if you notice it in time.
