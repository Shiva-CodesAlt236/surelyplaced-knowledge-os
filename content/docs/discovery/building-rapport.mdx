# Building Rapport

Rapport isn't small talk. It's not a personality trait. It's what happens when a candidate says something a little vulnerable and watches how you react — and you react like you actually heard it.

## Learning Objective

- Respond to an answer in a way that proves you were listening
- Use silence on purpose instead of filling it out of habit
- Notice the exact moment a candidate starts giving shorter, guarded answers

## Business Importance

- Rapport that slips mid-call quietly kills the quality of everything after it
- Candidates give shorter, safer answers the moment they feel unheard
- You usually can't tell rapport broke down in the moment — only later, when the answers get thin
- Guarded candidates trust competence before they trust warmth
- A candidate who feels genuinely heard tells you things that change your entire recommendation

## Real Conversation

**Candidate:**

Yeah, I was actually let go in March.

It's fine, it happens.

**Sales Executive:**

Can you say more about that?

I don't want to just note it and move on if there's more to it.

**Candidate:**

I mean...

It wasn't performance-related, they said restructuring.

But it still felt personal, you know? I was there four years.

**Sales Executive:**

That makes sense.

Four years is a long time — restructuring or not, that's going to feel personal no matter what they call it.

What was that actually like for you, in the weeks after?

**Candidate:**

Honestly?

Kind of destabilizing.

I started applying to literally anything. Wasn't even thinking about what I actually wanted.

**Sales Executive:**

That's a really common pattern.

And it's useful for me to know — because part of what we should figure out today is what you actually want.

Separate from what just felt safe to apply to, in the panic of it.

Does that sound right?

**Candidate:**

Yeah.

Yeah, actually — that's exactly it.

Nobody's asked me that so far.

**Sales Executive:**

I believe that. Most calls rush past this part.

We're not going to.

## Why Every Sentence Works

**"Can you say more about that?"**

Why?
Costs nothing to ask. Works almost anywhere in a conversation. Tells the candidate their answer mattered enough to go deeper — instead of just being logged and moved past.

**"I don't want to just note it and move on if there's more to it."**

Why?
Names the risk out loud — that you *could* brush past this — and explicitly chooses not to. That's more convincing than warmth alone.

**"What was that actually like for you?"**

Why?
Turns a fact ("I got laid off in March") into a story. Stories are where the real information — fear, pride, what they're actually looking for now — actually lives.

**"That's a really common pattern, and it's useful for me to know."**

Why?
Normalizes what the candidate just admitted, without minimizing it. "Common" doesn't mean "not a big deal" — it means "you're not alone in this, and I know what to do with it."

**"Does that sound right?"**

Why?
Hands the conclusion back to the candidate to confirm or correct. You're not telling them who they are — you're checking your read against theirs.

## Common Mistakes

❌ Moving straight to the next scripted question after a vulnerable answer
❌ Filling every silence immediately
❌ Saying "I totally understand exactly how you feel"
❌ Comparing them to another candidate's situation
❌ Using "can you say more about that" so often it stops sounding genuine

## Best Practices

- Acknowledge before you move on — every time
- Let a pause sit for one extra beat before you speak
- Ask what something was "actually like," not just what happened
- Give candidates a way to opt out of going deeper, without judgment
- Watch for answers getting shorter — that's the signal, not the tone of voice

## Roleplay

### Roleplay 1 — Easy

**Scenario**
Candidate is naturally chatty and opens up without much prompting.

**Candidate Profile**
Sneha, 24, recent grad, first job search, nervous but talkative.

**Conversation**

**Sales Executive:**
So what's been the hardest part of the search so far?

**Candidate:**
Honestly the silence. I apply and then... nothing. Not even a rejection email half the time.

**Decision Point**
Move straight to the next discovery question, or sit with this for one more beat?

**Best Response**
"That silence is honestly one of the worst parts of this whole process — most people don't say that part out loud. What's that been like, day to day?"

**Why**
Even an easy, talkative candidate deserves one beat of acknowledgment before you move forward. Skipping it trains even a chatty candidate to give you less over time.

### Roleplay 2 — Medium

**Scenario**
Candidate gives a short, slightly guarded answer partway through the call after opening up earlier.

**Candidate Profile**
Ankit, 28, mentioned a divorce in passing ten minutes ago, has gone quieter since.

**Conversation**

**Sales Executive:**
So, going back to timelines — how soon are you hoping to make a move?

**Candidate:**
I don't know. Soon I guess.

**Decision Point**
Take "I don't know, soon I guess" at face value and move on, or check in on the shift in tone?

**Best Response**
"I want to check in — is this pace working for you, or would it help to slow down a bit?"

**Why**
The answer got shorter, and that's the signal this lesson is about. Naming it directly, without pressure, is more likely to get rapport back than pretending not to notice.

### Roleplay 3 — Difficult

**Scenario**
Candidate shuts down a sensitive topic entirely and gets short with you.

**Candidate Profile**
Meera, 33, mentioned a "family situation" once and then refused to elaborate, tone turns cold.

**Conversation**

**Sales Executive:**
You mentioned a family situation earlier — is that something shaping your timeline at all?

**Candidate:**
I'd rather not get into that, honestly.

**Sales Executive:**
Of course — I hear you, and we don't need to.

**Candidate:**
...Okay. Thanks.

**Decision Point**
Circle back to the topic later in the call to "make sure you understood it right," or fully drop it?

**Best Response**
Fully drop it. Don't circle back unless she brings it up herself.

**Why**
Respecting a stated boundary completely — not "for now," not "let me just confirm" — is what actually rebuilds trust here. Circling back, even gently, reads as pushing past a "no."

## Practice Exercise

1. **Rewrite the opening** — A candidate just told you they're going through a rough patch at home. Write your next line.
2. **Respond to the objection** — Candidate says: "Why does this even matter for a job search?" after you ask what a layoff was like. Write your response.
3. **Continue the conversation** — Candidate gives a one-word answer ("Fine.") to a question that clearly deserves more. Write your next 3 lines.

## Cheat Sheet

- Acknowledge before you move forward, every single time
- "Can you say more about that?" works almost everywhere
- Ask what something was "actually like," not just what happened
- Offer an exit from sensitive topics — don't force depth
- Shorter answers mid-call = rapport slipping, check in
- Never say "I totally understand exactly how you feel"
- Never compare one candidate's situation to another's
- Let silence sit one extra beat before you fill it
- If they decline to elaborate, drop it completely — don't circle back
- Rapport is maintained the whole call, not built once at the start
