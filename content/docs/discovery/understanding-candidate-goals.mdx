# Understanding Candidate Goals

"I want to get into cloud" is not a goal. It's the first sentence of one. Find out what's underneath it before you build anything on top.

## Lesson Objective

- Tell the difference between a stated goal and the real need driving it
- Force prioritization when a candidate names several goals at once
- Surface constraints candidates don't volunteer unless asked directly

## Estimated Time

⏱ 8–10 minutes

## Conversation Timeline

| Time | Phase |
|---|---|
| 0:00–0:20 | Stated goal, surface-level |
| 0:20–0:50 | "Why that, specifically" — decision point |
| 0:50–1:20 | Real motivation surfaces |
| 1:20–1:45 | Reframe confirmed with candidate |

## Full Conversation Viewer

**Sales Executive (Aayush):**

What's the goal, in your own words?

**Candidate (Akash):**

I want to move into cloud engineering.

Seems like where the industry's heading.

**Sales Executive:**

Okay — why that, specifically?

What would it actually change for you if you got there?

**Candidate:**

I guess...

More job security? Cloud roles seem less likely to get cut.

## Decision Point

⏸ **PAUSE. What should Aayush say next?**

A. "Great, cloud is definitely in demand right now."
B. "That's useful. If you had to pick one thing this whole move needs to solve for you — is it the job security piece, or something else underneath that?"
C. "Okay, let's talk about which cloud certification track fits you."

## Correct Answer

**B.**

**Sales Executive:**

That's useful.

If you had to pick one thing this whole move needs to solve for you —

is it the job security piece, or is there something else underneath that?

**Candidate:**

Honestly, probably the security piece.

I got laid off two years ago and I still think about it more than I'd like to admit.

**Sales Executive:**

That makes a lot of sense, and I'm glad you said that directly — it changes how we should think about this.

Cloud engineering might genuinely be the right path. But the actual thing we need to solve for is stability, not the specific title.

Does that distinction feel right to you?

**Candidate:**

Yeah... yeah, actually, I hadn't thought about it that way. But that's exactly it.

## Coaching Notes

- Don't validate the surface goal ("cloud is in demand") before testing what's underneath it
- Let the layoff disclosure land fully — don't rush to the reframe
- End on a question, not a conclusion — "does that feel right" hands it back to him

## Why This Worked

- "Why that, specifically" is where a rehearsed answer cracks open
- Forcing a single priority surfaces what actually matters most
- Naming the reframe out loud, then checking it, avoids telling him who he is

## Common Mistakes

❌ Taking the first stated goal at face value
❌ Jumping to certification tracks before the real need is confirmed
❌ Treating "job security" as self-explanatory without asking why
❌ Moving to program discussion before the goal is genuinely tested

## Better Alternative

Weaker response: *"Okay, let's talk about which cloud certification track fits you."*

Why it's weaker: Skips straight to solutioning before the actual need is understood. If the real driver turns out to be stability rather than the cloud title specifically, this response has already locked onto the wrong target.

## Easy Roleplay

**Scenario:** Candidate gives a clear, specific goal with reasoning already attached.

**Candidate:** Rahul — wants to move from support into a data role, articulate about why.

**Conversation:**

**Sales Executive:** What's the goal, in your own words?

**Rahul:** I want to move into a data analyst role. I've been doing support for two years and I'm not learning anything new anymore.

**Decision Point:** Accept this as fully examined, or test it once?

**Best Response:** "That's a clear one. If you had to pick — is it more the stagnation, or specifically data as a field?"

**Why:** Even a well-articulated goal deserves one test question.

## Medium Roleplay

**Scenario:** Candidate names three goals in one breath, none prioritized.

**Candidate:** Priya — currently in QA, wants "more money, more respect, and something less repetitive."

**Conversation:**

**Sales Executive:** What's the goal, in your own words?

**Priya:** Honestly? More money, more respect, and something that's not so repetitive. All of it really.

**Decision Point:** Try to address all three, or force a single priority?

**Best Response:** "All fair. But if you genuinely could only have one of those right now — which one would you keep?"

**Why:** Multiple unprioritized goals produce a plan that doesn't actually target anything.

## Difficult Roleplay

**Scenario:** Candidate skips goals discovery entirely and demands pricing upfront.

**Candidate:** Emily — impatient, wants numbers before any discovery happens.

**Conversation:**

**Sales Executive:** What's the goal, in your own words?

**Emily:** Look, before we get into all that — what does this actually cost? I don't want to waste time if it's out of budget.

**Decision Point:** Give a number immediately, or hold the discovery sequence?

**Best Response:** "Fair question, and I'll get you real numbers before this call ends — I just don't want to quote you something without knowing what you actually need first, or it won't mean anything. Two minutes on your goal, then straight to pricing?"

**Why:** Refusing the question outright damages trust; answering it blind gives a meaningless number. Naming a specific, short delay — with a concrete promise to return to it — respects the request without abandoning discovery.

## Practice Exercise

1. Write your own version of "what's the goal, in your own words" for a candidate who already sounds impatient.
2. Candidate says: "Why do you need to know why? Just tell me if you can help." Write your response.
3. Candidate names two goals that seem to contradict each other. Write the next 3 lines helping them prioritize.

## Quick Reference Cheat Sheet

- Ask for the goal in their own words first
- "Why that, specifically?" is where the real answer surfaces
- Force a single priority when multiple goals show up
- Ask about constraints directly — timing, money, family
- Hold the first answer loosely until it's been tested once
- Never say "that's not really a good enough reason"
- Never promise a specific goal is guaranteed to be achieved
- A goal that shifts mid-call is often the truer one — name it gently
- If asked for pricing early, promise a specific return point — don't dodge or cave
