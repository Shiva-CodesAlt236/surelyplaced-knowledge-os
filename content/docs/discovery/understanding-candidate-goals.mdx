# Understanding Candidate Goals

"I want to get into cloud" is not a goal. It's the first sentence of one. Your job is to find out what's actually underneath it before you build anything on top.

## Learning Objective

- Tell the difference between a candidate's stated goal and the real need driving it
- Ask a question that forces prioritization when a candidate names several goals at once
- Surface constraints candidates don't volunteer unless asked directly

## Business Importance

- A recommendation built on the surface goal alone tends to fall apart later
- Candidates often lead with the goal that "sounds right," not the one that's actually true
- Constraints — money, family, timing — change what's realistic, and rarely get volunteered
- A goal that survives being questioned is worth building a plan around. One that doesn't, isn't
- Getting this wrong means the candidate gets exactly what they asked for, and it still doesn't fix anything

## Real Conversation

**Sales Executive:**

What's the goal, in your own words?

**Candidate:**

I want to move into cloud engineering.

Seems like where the industry's heading.

**Sales Executive:**

Okay — why that, specifically?

What would it actually change for you if you got there?

**Candidate:**

I guess...

More job security? Cloud roles seem less likely to get cut.

**Sales Executive:**

That's useful.

If you had to pick one thing this whole move needs to solve for you —

is it the job security piece, or is there something else underneath that?

**Candidate:**

Honestly, probably the security piece.

I got laid off two years ago and I still think about it more than I'd like to admit.

**Sales Executive:**

That makes a lot of sense. And I'm glad you said that directly, because it changes how we should think about this.

Cloud engineering might genuinely be the right path.

But the actual thing we need to solve for is stability — not the specific title.

That might mean cloud. Or it might mean something else that gets you there faster or more reliably.

Does that distinction feel right to you?

**Candidate:**

Yeah...

Yeah, actually, I hadn't thought about it that way.

But that's exactly it.

## Why Every Sentence Works

**"What's the goal, in your own words?"**

Why?
Invites the rehearsed answer on purpose. You need to hear it before you can work past it — don't skip to the "real" answer, the stated one is still useful data.

**"Why that, specifically? What would it actually change for you?"**

Why?
This is where the rehearsed answer usually cracks open. Forces the candidate to connect the goal to a real consequence in their life — that's where actual motivation lives.

**"If you had to pick one thing this needs to solve for you — what would it be?"**

Why?
Forces prioritization. Candidates often name several goals at once without realizing some matter far more than others.

**"I'm glad you said that directly, because it changes how we should think about this."**

Why?
Confirms the disclosure mattered and immediately puts it to use — not just acknowledged, but visibly acted on.

**"Does that distinction feel right to you?"**

Why?
Hands the reframe back to the candidate to confirm. You're not telling them what they really want — you're checking your read against theirs.

## Common Mistakes

❌ Taking the first stated goal at face value
❌ Treating constraints as an afterthought instead of core information
❌ Pushing past a vague answer instead of asking a sharper follow-up
❌ Assuming every real motivation is financial
❌ Moving to program discussion before the goal is genuinely confirmed

## Best Practices

- Ask "why that, specifically" — curious tone, not skeptical
- Force a choice when multiple goals show up at once
- Ask about constraints directly — they rarely get volunteered
- Hold the first stated goal loosely until it's been tested once
- Name a shift in the goal gently if you notice one mid-call

## Roleplay

### Roleplay 1 — Easy

**Scenario**
Candidate gives a clear, specific goal on the first try, with real reasoning already attached.

**Candidate Profile**
Rohan, 26, wants to move from support into a data role, already articulate about why.

**Conversation**

**Sales Executive:**
What's the goal, in your own words?

**Candidate:**
I want to move into a data analyst role. I've been doing support for two years and I'm just not learning anything new anymore.

**Decision Point**
Accept this as fully examined, or still test it once?

**Best Response**
"That's a clear one. If you had to pick — is it more about the stagnation, or specifically about data as a field?"

**Why**
Even a well-articulated goal deserves one test question. It either confirms the goal is solid or surfaces something underneath it you'd have otherwise missed.

### Roleplay 2 — Medium

**Scenario**
Candidate names three different goals in one breath, none prioritized.

**Candidate Profile**
Ankit, 32, currently in a help desk role, mentions wanting "more money, more respect, and something less boring" all at once.

**Conversation**

**Sales Executive:**
What's the goal, in your own words?

**Candidate:**
Honestly? More money, more respect, and something that's not this boring. All of it, really.

**Decision Point**
Try to address all three, or force a single priority?

**Best Response**
"All fair. But if you genuinely could only have one of those right now — which one would you keep?"

**Why**
Multiple unprioritized goals produce a vague, unbuildable plan. Forcing a single choice gets you the one that actually matters most, without dismissing the others.

### Roleplay 3 — Difficult

**Scenario**
Candidate insists their stated goal is the whole story and gets defensive when questioned further.

**Candidate Profile**
Neha, 35, insists she "just wants to switch to product management," gets irritated when asked to go deeper, feels like she's being doubted.

**Conversation**

**Sales Executive:**
Why product management, specifically — what would it change for you?

**Candidate:**
I don't know, does it matter? I just want it. Do you need a whole essay to help me?

**Sales Executive:**
Fair pushback — no essay needed. I ask because the "why" usually changes what path actually gets you there fastest. That's it.

**Candidate:**
...Okay. I guess I'm tired of being the person whose ideas get ignored in meetings.

**Decision Point**
Apologize for pushing, or acknowledge the answer and keep moving?

**Best Response**
Acknowledge it plainly and move forward — no apology needed for asking a reasonable question.

**Why**
Over-apologizing here would suggest the question was inappropriate, when it wasn't. A short, respectful explanation of *why* you asked — followed by taking the real answer seriously — rebuilds the exchange without conceding ground that didn't need conceding.

## Practice Exercise

1. **Rewrite the opening** — Write your own version of "what's the goal, in your own words" for a candidate who already sounds impatient.
2. **Respond to the objection** — Candidate says: "Why do you need to know why? Just tell me if you can help." Write your response.
3. **Continue the conversation** — Candidate names two goals that seem to contradict each other. Write the next 3 lines helping them prioritize.

## Cheat Sheet

- Ask for the goal in their own words first — don't skip it
- "Why that, specifically?" is where the real answer surfaces
- Force a single priority when multiple goals show up
- Ask about constraints directly — timing, money, family
- Hold the first answer loosely until it's been tested once
- Never say "that's not really a good enough reason"
- Never say "most people say that, but what they really mean is..."
- Never promise a specific goal is guaranteed to be achieved
- A goal that shifts mid-call is often the truer one — name it gently
- Confirm the reframed goal back with the candidate before moving on
