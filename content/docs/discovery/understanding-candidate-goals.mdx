# Understanding Candidate Goals

## Purpose

Qualification tells you whether someone can plausibly be helped. This article is about the next layer: figuring out what they actually want, which is very often not the same thing as what they say in the first thirty seconds of being asked. Candidates usually lead with a stated goal — "I want to get into cloud" or "I want a better title" — and the advisor's job is to find out what's underneath that goal before treating it as the whole answer.

This matters more than it might sound like it should. A recommendation built on a candidate's surface-level stated goal, without understanding what's actually driving it, tends to fall apart later — either the candidate loses conviction partway through, or they get exactly what they asked for and it doesn't actually solve the problem they were carrying.

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish between a candidate's stated goal and the need actually driving it
- Ask questions that surface constraints candidates don't volunteer on their own
- Recognize when a stated goal is really a proxy for something else — status, security, recognition, escape from a bad situation
- Avoid locking in on the first goal a candidate mentions without checking it against what else comes up later in the call
- Reflect a goal back to a candidate in a way that either confirms it or invites a more honest version

## Candidate Psychology

Candidates often arrive at a discovery call with a goal they've already rehearsed — sometimes because they think it's what they're supposed to say, sometimes because it's genuinely what they believe they want, and sometimes because naming a specific, ambitious goal feels safer than naming the vaguer, more vulnerable thing actually driving them.

It's worth understanding a common pattern here: a stated goal like "I want to move into cloud engineering" is often standing in for something else — wanting to feel technically respected again after being stuck doing unglamorous work, wanting more job security after watching colleagues get laid off, wanting to prove something to family who questioned an earlier career decision. The stated goal isn't false. It's just often incomplete, and the incomplete version is much harder to build an honest recommendation around.

There's also a real risk of candidates stating a goal that reflects where they think they *should* be aiming rather than where they actually want to be — shaped by what looks impressive on LinkedIn, or what a friend achieved, more than by genuine interest. Good discovery gently tests for this without ever implying the candidate's stated goal is wrong or naive.

## Advisor Objective

Get past the first, rehearsed version of a candidate's goal to the actual need underneath it, and confirm that need directly with the candidate before treating any specific target role, timeline, or outcome as settled.

## When To Use

- After qualification is confirmed, as the natural next stage of discovery
- Any time a candidate states a goal quickly and confidently, almost too smoothly — that fluency is sometimes a sign the goal has been rehearsed more than examined
- When a candidate's stated goal doesn't seem to match other things they've said earlier in the call
- Before any discussion of specific tracks, roles, or program fit — goals should be genuinely understood first

## Discovery Questions

1. "What's the goal, in your own words — what are you actually trying to get to?"
2. "Why that, specifically — what would it change for you if you got there?"
3. "If you had to pick just one thing this needs to solve for you, what would it be?"
4. "Is there anything about your current situation — timing, finances, family, location — that shapes what's actually realistic for you right now?"
5. "If I asked the people closest to you why you're doing this, what would they say?"

## Why Each Question Matters

The first question invites the rehearsed answer, on purpose — you need to hear it before you can work past it. Don't skip this step trying to get straight to the "real" answer; the stated goal is still useful information, even if it's incomplete.

The second question is where the rehearsed answer usually starts to crack open into something more specific and more honest. "What would it change for you" forces the candidate to connect the goal to an actual consequence in their life, which is where the real motivation tends to live.

The third question forces prioritization, which is useful because candidates often name several goals at once without realizing some matter far more than others. Getting them to pick one thing gives you a much clearer target to work with.

The fourth question surfaces constraints that shape what's realistic — and candidates frequently don't volunteer these unless asked directly, either because they don't think to mention them or because they don't want to sound like they're already making excuses.

The fifth question is a reframing tool. Asking a candidate to imagine how someone else would describe their motivation often produces a more honest, less rehearsed answer than asking them directly, because it removes a layer of self-consciousness.

## How to Interpret Candidate Answers

If a candidate's answer to the second question is concrete and specific ("it would mean I finally stop worrying about being the first one let go if there are cuts"), you've found something real and important — build the rest of the conversation around it, and check any later recommendation against it directly.

If the answer stays abstract or repeats the same phrasing as the first answer almost word for word, the candidate likely hasn't examined the goal much yet — that's not a problem, it just means more of this conversation needs to happen before any track or recommendation gets discussed.

Listen closely to the third question for goals that surprise you — sometimes a candidate will surface something they hadn't mentioned at all yet ("honestly, more than the money, I just want to feel competent again"). Treat a surprising answer as more reliable, not less, than the polished opening one.

On the fourth question, pay attention to anything mentioned quickly or almost apologetically — candidates sometimes downplay real constraints (a tight financial runway, a spouse who isn't fully on board yet) because they don't want to seem like they're bringing problems to the conversation. These are exactly the things worth taking seriously.

## Follow-up Questions

- If the goal stays abstract after question two: "Can you paint me a specific picture — six months from now, what does a normal Tuesday actually look like if this works out?"
- If a candidate names multiple goals in question three: "If you genuinely could only have one of those, which one would you keep?"
- If a constraint surfaces in question four: "How much does that constraint actually limit what's possible, versus just making it harder?"
- If question five reveals something different from the stated goal: "That's interesting — does that match how you'd describe it, or does it feel a little different?"

## Common Mistakes

- Taking the first stated goal at face value and building the rest of the call around it without testing it further
- Treating constraint questions as an afterthought instead of a core part of understanding what's actually achievable
- Pushing past a vague or rehearsed-sounding answer instead of asking a follow-up that invites something more specific
- Assuming every candidate's real motivation is financial, when it's frequently about security, recognition, or self-worth instead
- Moving to program discussion before confirming the goal directly with the candidate in their own words

## Things Never To Say

- "That's not really a good enough reason." (No stated motivation is wrong — some are just less examined than others, and the job is to help examine it, not judge it.)
- "Most people say that, but what they really mean is..." (Presuming to know a candidate's real motivation better than they do, without asking, undermines the whole discovery process.)
- Any promise tying a specific goal to a guaranteed outcome — we diagnose and recommend; we don't guarantee that a stated goal will be achieved.

## Conversation Example

> **Advisor:** "What's the goal, in your own words?"
> **Candidate:** "I want to move into cloud engineering. It seems like where the industry's heading."
> **Advisor:** "Okay — why that, specifically? What would it actually change for you if you got there?"
> **Candidate:** "I guess... more job security? Cloud roles seem less likely to get cut."
> **Advisor:** "That's useful. If you had to pick one thing this whole move needs to solve for you, is it the job security piece, or is there something else underneath that?"
> **Candidate:** "Honestly, probably the security piece. I got laid off two years ago and I still think about it more than I'd like to admit."
> **Advisor:** "That makes a lot of sense, and I'm glad you said that directly — because it changes how we should think about this. Cloud engineering might genuinely be the right path, but the actual thing we need to solve for is stability, not the specific title. That might mean cloud, or it might mean something else that gets you there faster or more reliably. Does that distinction feel right to you?"
> **Candidate:** "Yeah... yeah, actually, I hadn't thought about it that way, but that's exactly it."

## Advanced Advisor Tips

Experienced advisors learn to hold a stated goal loosely until it's been tested at least once, without ever implying to the candidate that their first answer wasn't good enough. The skill is in the phrasing — questions like "why that, specifically" land as curious, not skeptical, when the tone is right.

It's also worth paying attention to goals that shift slightly over the course of a single call. A candidate who opens with "senior cloud engineer" and later says something closer to "I just want to stop feeling behind" has told you two different things, and the second one is very likely closer to the truth. Don't force a false consistency between the two — name the shift gently and ask which one feels more accurate.

## Summary

A candidate's first stated goal is real, but it's rarely the whole story. Ask what it would actually change for them, force prioritization when multiple goals show up at once, and take constraints seriously even when they're mentioned quickly or reluctantly. The goal worth building a recommendation around is the one that survives being questioned, not the one that came out fastest.
