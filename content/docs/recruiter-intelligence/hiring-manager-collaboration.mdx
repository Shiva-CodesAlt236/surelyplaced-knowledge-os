# Hiring Manager Collaboration

## Purpose

A candidate sometimes gets a warm signal from a recruiter and a cooler one from a hiring manager, or the reverse, and isn't sure what to make of the mismatch. This article explains why that happens as a normal feature of how these two roles collaborate, not a sign something has gone wrong.

## Scope

This article covers the general working relationship between a recruiter and a hiring manager during a process. It does not claim to know how any specific pair actually communicates, and it does not suggest a candidate can or should try to manage that relationship directly.

## Core Content

A recruiter and a hiring manager typically have different vantage points on the same role. The hiring manager usually owns the underlying need — what the team actually requires, day to day — while the recruiter usually owns the process of finding and moving candidates through it. Neither has complete visibility into the other's full reasoning at every moment, which is a normal feature of the collaboration, not a breakdown in it.

Because of this, a recruiter's read on a candidate's fit is often provisional until it's been checked against the hiring manager's own impression, and a hiring manager's enthusiasm doesn't always translate immediately into next steps if the recruiter is still coordinating scheduling, other candidates in the pipeline, or internal approvals. A candidate reading too much into either signal in isolation risks a false conclusion in either direction.

Where a genuine mismatch or a hiring manager change happens mid-process, that's something that occasionally occurs in ordinary hiring and doesn't necessarily reflect anything about the candidate — this article doesn't attempt to explain any specific instance of it, since the actual cause is internal to the employer and not something this repository can know.

## Advisor Guidance

Help candidates avoid over-reading a single signal from either the recruiter or the hiring manager in isolation. Frame a mismatch or a slower-than-expected step as an ordinary feature of two people coordinating, not evidence of a problem specific to the candidate.

## Key Takeaways

A recruiter and hiring manager operate from different vantage points on the same role, so a mismatched signal between them is a normal feature of that collaboration rather than a red flag — a candidate is better served understanding that than reading too much into either signal alone.
