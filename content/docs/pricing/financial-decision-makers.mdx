# Financial Decision-Makers

> This is a sales playbook, not a pricing or negotiation document. This article does not state or imply any specific price, discount, or financing term. It focuses on the psychology and process of bringing a financial co-decision-maker into an honest, well-informed conversation.

## Purpose

[Parents / Spouse Approval](/docs/objections/parents-spouse-approval) covers the general objection pattern of a candidate needing someone else's approval. This article goes deeper into a related but distinct situation: when the person the candidate needs to involve isn't just an approval-giver, but a genuine co-owner of the household's financial decisions. Treating that person as an obstacle to route around, rather than a legitimate second stakeholder in an honest conversation, tends to backfire — and this article covers how to include them well.

## Learning Objectives

- Recognize the difference between a candidate needing "approval" and a candidate having a genuine financial co-decision-maker
- Understand why excluding a real co-decision-maker from the conversation tends to weaken the eventual decision rather than speed it up
- Offer to include a financial co-decision-maker directly, rather than leaving the candidate to relay everything secondhand
- Maintain the same honesty and non-invention standards from this module when a second person is present
- Recognize when involving a co-decision-maker surfaces a deeper affordability concern that belongs in [Pricing Red Flags](/docs/pricing/pricing-red-flags)

## Candidate Mindset

Candidates vary widely in how they describe this. Some say plainly, early on, that a spouse or parent is part of every financial decision in the household — this is simply how the household operates, not a sign of hesitation. Others mention it more reluctantly, sometimes because they worry it will make them look less independent or less ready to decide. A candidate who raises this honestly and early is usually not stalling — they're describing their actual decision-making process.

## Advisor Mindset

The temptation to treat a financial co-decision-maker as friction to be minimized — "just relay this to them" — misreads the situation. A decision made by one household member and then explained secondhand to another is a weaker, more fragile decision than one made with both people present for the honest version of the conversation. Inviting the co-decision-maker in directly is usually the stronger move, not a concession.

## When To Use

- Any time a candidate mentions that a spouse, partner, parent, or other person is part of the household's financial decisions
- When a candidate seems to be relaying information secondhand to someone not on the call, and that relay seems to be losing important nuance
- When a decision stalls specifically around financial commitment and a second stakeholder hasn't yet been included directly

## Recommended Conversation Framework

Normalize the situation immediately — households make financial decisions together all the time, and this is not treated as a delay tactic or a weaker form of commitment.

Offer directly to include the co-decision-maker in a future conversation, rather than leaving the candidate to explain everything alone. Frame this as making the eventual decision stronger, not as adding a step.

If a joint conversation happens, hold the same standards from the rest of this module — no invented figures, no different story told to the second person than was told to the first, complete honesty about what is and isn't confirmed.

Watch for whether the co-decision-maker's concerns are practical (timing, structure) or reflect a genuine affordability limit — the latter belongs in [Pricing Red Flags](/docs/pricing/pricing-red-flags), not further persuasion.

## Discovery Questions

1. "Is this a decision you're making on your own, or is someone else part of your household's financial decisions too?"
2. "Would it be helpful to have them on a call directly, so they hear everything the same way you did?"
3. "What questions do you think they'd want answered that we haven't covered yet?"
4. "If they had a concern about this, what do you think it would be?"

## Buying Signals

- The candidate raises a co-decision-maker early and matter-of-factly, consistent with a genuine household process rather than hesitation
- The candidate welcomes the offer to include the co-decision-maker directly, rather than resisting it
- The co-decision-maker, once included, asks specific and practical questions rather than expressing broad skepticism

## Risk Signals

- The candidate seems to be relaying a very different version of the conversation to the co-decision-maker than what was actually discussed
- The co-decision-maker's concerns, once heard directly, sound like genuine affordability strain rather than ordinary due diligence — see [Pricing Red Flags](/docs/pricing/pricing-red-flags)
- The candidate resists including the co-decision-maker even after repeated stalls that seem to trace back to that person

## Common Mistakes

- Treating a financial co-decision-maker as an obstacle to be minimized rather than a legitimate second stakeholder
- Telling the co-decision-maker a different or softened version of something already discussed with the candidate
- Failing to offer a direct conversation and instead leaving the candidate to relay everything alone
- Assuming every mention of a spouse or parent is a stalling tactic rather than checking which it actually is

## Ethical Guidelines

A financial co-decision-maker deserves the same honest, complete information the primary candidate received — never a simplified or more persuasive version designed to overcome their specific concerns. If their honest reaction, once fully informed, is hesitation or a no, that is a legitimate outcome of an honest process, not a problem to be solved through a different message.

## Key Takeaways

A genuine financial co-decision-maker is not an obstacle — including them directly and honestly tends to produce a stronger, more durable decision than leaving the candidate to relay the conversation alone. Normalize their involvement, offer to include them directly, and hold the same honesty standard for both people in the room.
