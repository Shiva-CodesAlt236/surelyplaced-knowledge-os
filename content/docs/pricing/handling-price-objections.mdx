# Handling Price Objections

> This is a sales playbook, not a pricing document. This article does not state or imply any specific price, discount, or financing term anywhere. See [Price Objection](/docs/objections/price-objection) for the core objection-handling framework this article builds on — this article goes a layer deeper into the specific shapes a price objection can take.

## Purpose

[Price Objection](/docs/objections/price-objection) covers the core framework for responding to cost-related pushback. This article goes further: price objections aren't one thing, and the specific shape an objection takes tells you something important about what's actually going on. This article breaks down the common variants and how investment psychology (see [Investment Psychology](/docs/pricing/investment-psychology)) applies differently to each.

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish between at least four distinct shapes a price objection commonly takes
- Match each variant to the psychological pattern most likely driving it
- Respond to each variant with a genuinely different approach, rather than one generic price response
- Recognize when a price objection is actually a proxy for a different, unstated concern
- Avoid treating a comparison to a cheaper alternative as automatically resolvable through argument

## Candidate Mindset

A candidate saying "it's too expensive" could mean any of several different things, and the words alone rarely distinguish between them. Sometimes it's a straightforward affordability constraint. Sometimes it's a comparison against a cheaper, and possibly quite different, alternative. Sometimes it's genuine uncertainty about whether the value justifies the cost. And sometimes, as covered in [Investment Psychology](/docs/pricing/investment-psychology), it's guilt or fear expressing itself through the most socially acceptable channel — the number.

## Advisor Mindset

The instinct to respond to "it's too expensive" with a single, rehearsed rebuttal is strong, but it treats a genuinely varied set of underlying concerns as if they were one thing. An advisor's real skill here is diagnostic, not persuasive — figuring out which variant is actually in play before responding, rather than responding to the words alone.

## When To Use

- Any time a candidate raises a cost objection, as a way of identifying which specific variant is actually present
- When a rehearsed price response doesn't seem to be landing, which often means the wrong variant was assumed
- When training a newer advisor to move past a single, generic price rebuttal

## Recommended Conversation Framework

**The affordability objection** ("I genuinely can't afford this right now") deserves the most direct honesty. If this is real, it's real — respect it, and don't try to reframe genuine financial constraint as a mindset problem to be talked through.

**The comparison objection** ("X is cheaper") deserves a conversation about what's actually being compared, not a defensive reaction. Ask what the candidate understands about what the cheaper alternative includes, and let genuine differences speak for themselves rather than disparaging the other option.

**The value-uncertainty objection** ("I'm not sure this is worth it") deserves a return to the specific value already discussed, reconnected to the candidate's own goals — see [ROI Conversations](/docs/pricing/roi-conversations) for how to do this without ever citing a specific figure.

**The proxy objection** (cost is standing in for guilt, fear, or a deeper unresolved doubt) deserves the approach laid out in [Investment Psychology](/docs/pricing/investment-psychology) — acknowledging the emotional layer directly rather than continuing to argue about the number.

## Discovery Questions

1. "When you say it feels like too much, is that about the number itself, or about where things stand for you financially right now?"
2. "You mentioned another option — what do you know about what that actually includes?"
3. "Is it more that the cost feels high, or that you're not yet sure it's worth it — those are different things, and I want to respond to the right one."
4. "Is there something underneath this that feels bigger than the number itself?"

## Buying Signals

- The candidate engages specifically with the distinction being asked about, rather than repeating the same vague objection
- Once the correct variant is identified and addressed, the candidate's tone shifts toward genuine consideration rather than continued deflection
- The candidate volunteers more detail once they sense the advisor is trying to understand the real concern, not just overcome an objection

## Risk Signals

- The candidate's objection doesn't resolve no matter which variant is addressed, suggesting the real concern hasn't surfaced yet
- The affordability objection seems to reflect genuine financial strain rather than ordinary hesitation — see [Pricing Red Flags](/docs/pricing/pricing-red-flags)
- The candidate becomes more guarded, not less, as the conversation continues, which may signal the approach itself needs to change

## Common Mistakes

- Responding to every price objection with the same rehearsed answer regardless of which variant is actually present
- Treating a genuine affordability constraint as something to be reasoned past
- Disparaging a cheaper alternative instead of having an honest, specific comparison
- Missing a proxy objection and continuing to argue about the number when the real issue is emotional

## Ethical Guidelines

Diagnosing the type of objection is meant to produce a more honest, relevant response — never a more effective way to pressure a reluctant candidate. If the honest answer, once the real concern is understood, is that this isn't the right time or fit, that's a legitimate outcome, not a failure to find the right rebuttal.

## Key Takeaways

"It's too expensive" can mean genuine affordability strain, an unfair comparison, real uncertainty about value, or an emotional concern wearing a financial costume — and each deserves a different, honest response. Diagnose which one is actually present before responding, and never use that diagnosis to pressure rather than understand.
