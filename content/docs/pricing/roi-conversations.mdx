# ROI Conversations

> This is a sales playbook, not a pricing or outcomes document. This article does not state or imply any salary figure, payback period, timeline-to-return, or placement statistic anywhere, and it never will. Return on investment is discussed here in qualitative, structural terms only — never as a number.

## Purpose

Candidates evaluating a real financial commitment naturally think in terms of return: what do I get back for what I put in? This is a completely reasonable frame, and this article covers how to have that conversation honestly — connecting cost to real, structural value — without ever crossing into stating a specific dollar return, timeline, or outcome that can't be honestly promised. The discipline in this article is as much about what not to say as what to say.

## Learning Objectives

- Frame value in structural, qualitative terms rather than quantified outcome claims
- Connect the cost of the program to a candidate's own specific goals rather than a generic value proposition
- Recognize the moment a "what's the ROI" question is being asked, and redirect it honestly rather than inventing a number to satisfy it
- Distinguish between describing what's provided and promising what it will produce
- Hold this discipline even when a candidate directly asks for a number

## Candidate Mindset

Many candidates, especially those from analytically-minded fields, will ask directly about ROI, sometimes using exactly that language. This is a completely reasonable question from their side — they're trying to make a sound financial decision, the same way they might evaluate any other investment. The instinct to give them a satisfying, concrete number in response is strong, precisely because it's what they're asking for.

It's worth understanding that a candidate who asks this question is often more reassured by an honest "here's why I can't give you a specific number, and here's what I can tell you instead" than by a confident-sounding figure that turns out to be invented or unverifiable. Sophisticated evaluators tend to distrust numbers that arrive too easily.

## Advisor Mindset

The discipline required here is significant: resisting the pull to answer a direct, reasonable-sounding question with a number that would make the conversation easier in the moment, but that isn't something Surely Placed can honestly stand behind. This isn't about withholding information — it's about not inventing information that doesn't exist, or that varies too much by individual circumstance to state responsibly.

## When To Use

- Any time a candidate asks directly about return on investment, payback period, or expected outcome
- When presenting value in a discussion call, as a way of avoiding accidental drift into implied guarantees
- When a candidate compares this decision to a financial investment and expects investment-style figures in response

## Recommended Conversation Framework

When a candidate asks for a specific ROI figure, acknowledge the question as reasonable, and be direct that a genuine, individual figure isn't something that can be responsibly given — outcomes depend on factors specific to each candidate and circumstances outside any program's control (see [No Guarantee Concern](/docs/objections/no-guarantee-concern)).

Redirect to what can be honestly described: the structural value provided — preparation, positioning, access, support — connected specifically to what the candidate has already said they're trying to achieve.

If the candidate presses for a number anyway, hold the line calmly rather than relenting to make the moment more comfortable. Explain that a made-up number would actually serve them worse than an honest "I can't respectably give you one."

## Discovery Questions

1. "When you think about return on this, what would actually feel like a return to you — is it about compensation, stability, satisfaction, or something else?"
2. "How are you currently thinking about weighing this decision financially?"
3. "Is there a specific number you were expecting, and can I explain honestly why I'm not going to give you one?"
4. "What would make this feel like a clearly sound decision to you, beyond a specific figure?"

## Buying Signals

- The candidate accepts the honest explanation for why a specific figure isn't available, and the conversation continues productively
- The candidate engages with the qualitative value described rather than continuing to press only for a number
- The candidate's own definition of "return" (from the first discovery question) becomes something the advisor can speak to concretely, without inventing a figure

## Risk Signals

- The candidate seems to be evaluating this decision entirely on the presence or absence of a specific promised figure
- The candidate references a number from another program or source that sounds like an outcome guarantee, worth addressing honestly rather than competing with
- The advisor notices their own temptation to offer a number just to move the conversation forward

## Common Mistakes

- Offering a specific ROI figure, timeline, or outcome statistic to satisfy a candidate's direct question
- Implying a guarantee indirectly, through confident phrasing, even while avoiding the word "guarantee" itself
- Failing to connect qualitative value back to the candidate's own specific goals, leaving the value description generic
- Getting flustered or evasive when asked directly for a number, instead of explaining calmly why one isn't given

## Ethical Guidelines

Never state or imply a specific financial return, outcome timeline, or placement statistic under any circumstance, including under direct, repeated candidate pressure. Honesty about the limits of what can be promised is not a weaker sales position — it's the only ethical one available, and it's also, in practice, usually the more credible one to a careful evaluator.

## Key Takeaways

Return on investment is a fair question and deserves an honest answer — but an honest answer here is structural and qualitative, never a specific figure, timeline, or guarantee. Redirect toward what can genuinely be described, connect it to the candidate's own goals, and hold that line calmly even under direct pressure for a number.
