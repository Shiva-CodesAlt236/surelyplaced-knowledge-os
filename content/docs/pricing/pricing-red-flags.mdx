# Pricing Red Flags

> This is a sales playbook, not a pricing document. This article does not state any figure, discount, or policy. Its purpose is to help advisors recognize when a financial conversation needs care, honesty, and possibly escalation — not persuasion.

## Purpose

Most cost-related hesitation covered elsewhere in this module — [Handling Price Objections](/docs/pricing/handling-price-objections), [Investment Psychology](/docs/pricing/investment-psychology) — is ordinary and workable through honest conversation. This article covers the smaller set of situations where a financial conversation has crossed into something that deserves a different response: genuine financial vulnerability, or a request that sits outside what an advisor is positioned to answer alone. Recognizing the difference matters more than any persuasion technique.

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish ordinary price hesitation from genuine financial vulnerability
- Recognize signals that a candidate may be considering a decision they can't actually afford
- Know when a financial question needs to be escalated rather than answered directly
- Avoid using persuasion skill to push past a legitimate red flag
- Respond to a red flag with care rather than treating it as an objection to be overcome

## Candidate Mindset

A candidate under real financial strain doesn't always say so directly — sometimes it shows up as unusual urgency, sometimes as vague answers to direct questions about affordability, sometimes as a willingness to say yes that feels disconnected from the hesitation shown earlier in the conversation. The words used in an ordinary objection and the words used when something more serious is happening can look similar on the surface, which is exactly why this distinction requires attention rather than a script.

## Advisor Mindset

An advisor's job when a genuine red flag appears is not to close the sale more skillfully — it's to slow down, ask honestly, and, in some cases, gently discourage a decision that doesn't look sound for the candidate. This runs counter to typical sales instinct, but it's consistent with the consultative, ethical approach this entire module is built on. A short-term close that leaves a candidate in a worse financial position is not a success by any definition that matters here.

## When To Use

- Any time a candidate's financial situation, urgency, or answers to direct questions raise genuine concern rather than ordinary hesitation
- Before treating any "yes" as fully informed and voluntary, particularly when it arrives faster or with less deliberation than the rest of the conversation would suggest
- When a candidate's question falls outside standard pricing conversation and touches something an advisor isn't positioned to answer alone, such as a legal, immigration, or highly specific financial question

## Recommended Conversation Framework

Slow the conversation down rather than moving toward close. Ask directly and kindly about what's driving the urgency or the hesitation, without assuming the answer.

If genuine financial strain becomes apparent, say so honestly rather than continuing to sell — a direct, caring "it sounds like this might not be the right time financially, and that's okay" is a legitimate and appropriate response.

If a question requires authority or expertise the advisor doesn't have — discount approval, legal or immigration specifics, an unusual financial arrangement — escalate it through the correct internal channel rather than answering from best judgment. See [Discount Guidelines](/docs/pricing/discount-guidelines) for the discount-specific version of this same discipline.

## Discovery Questions

1. "I want to make sure this is the right decision for you financially right now — can you tell me honestly how this fits into your bigger picture?"
2. "Is there something time-sensitive driving the urgency here, or is that coming from somewhere else?"
3. "Is there anything about the financial side of this that still feels uncertain or uncomfortable, even if it hasn't come up yet?"
4. "If you take a step back, does this decision feel sound to you, independent of anything I've said?"

## Buying Signals

Not the primary focus of this article — a "buying signal" in the traditional sense is less relevant here than an honest read of whether the decision is genuinely sound for the candidate. When a candidate's financial picture is confirmed to be stable and the decision reflects real, considered readiness, that is itself the positive signal this article is looking for.

## Risk Signals

- Urgency that seems disconnected from the candidate's actual circumstances or timeline
- Vague, deflecting, or inconsistent answers to direct, kind questions about affordability
- A candidate willing to commit financially in a way that seems to conflict with what they've said elsewhere about their situation
- A question that requires discount authority, legal expertise, or immigration guidance the advisor doesn't have — see [Discount Guidelines](/docs/pricing/discount-guidelines)
- A financial co-decision-maker who has been deliberately kept out of the conversation — see [Financial Decision-Makers](/docs/pricing/financial-decision-makers)

## Common Mistakes

- Treating a genuine financial red flag as just another objection to be persuaded past
- Moving toward close specifically because a candidate seems eager, without checking whether that eagerness is well-founded
- Answering a legal, immigration, or discount-authority question directly instead of escalating it
- Feeling that slowing down or discouraging a decision represents a personal or professional failure, rather than the correct outcome in that situation

## Ethical Guidelines

An advisor's responsibility to the candidate's genuine wellbeing outranks any pressure, real or perceived, to close a sale. When a real financial or authority-related red flag appears, respond with honesty and care, not persuasion. Escalating a question is a sign of good judgment, not a shortfall — never answer past the edge of confirmed knowledge or authority.

Most price hesitation is ordinary and workable through honest conversation, but a smaller set of situations — genuine financial strain, disconnected urgency, or a question beyond advisor authority — call for care and escalation instead of persuasion. Recognizing that distinction, and acting on it honestly, matters more than any closing skill.
