# Sales Academy Content Framework

**Status:** Canonical reference — the writing bible
**Applies to:** Every lesson written for `content/docs/discovery`, `discussion`, `closing`, `objections`, `pricing`, `sales-coaching`, `sales-constitution`, and every future Sales Academy module
**Owner:** Chief Learning Officer / Documentation Architect function
**Last updated:** 2026-08-05

This document is the permanent writing standard for the Sales Academy specifically. It sits alongside, and does not replace, `docs/KNOWLEDGE_OS_DOCUMENTATION_STANDARD.md`, which remains canonical for the broader Knowledge OS content tree (Candidate Intelligence and the `*-intelligence` modules) and for the underlying non-invention disciplines — no invented pricing, guarantees, statistics, or policy — that this document inherits without restating in full. What this document adds is specific to the Sales Academy's evolved, conversation-first lesson shape, which has grown beyond the general seven-section article standard into its own widget-based format. Every future lesson, written by any future writer or AI agent, must follow this document. Where a future instruction conflicts with it, the conflict should be raised, not silently resolved either way.

## Purpose

A Sales Academy lesson exists to make one specific, real skill land through a worked example, then let the learner attempt it themselves at increasing difficulty. It is not reference documentation to be consulted mid-call the way a Knowledge OS article is; it is teaching material meant to be read once, deeply, and internalized. Every structural rule in this document exists in service of that purpose.

## Learning Outcomes

Every lesson states, through its Lesson Objective, what a learner should be able to do differently after reading it — never what they should merely know. A learning outcome is phrased as a capability ("recognize the difference between a resolved and a dropped objection"), never as a fact to memorize. A lesson with no clear behavioral outcome is a sign the lesson is trying to teach reference material rather than a skill, and should be reconsidered as Knowledge OS content instead.

## Conversation Standards

Every lesson is built around at least one real, worked conversation rendered through the Conversation Viewer widget, and that conversation is the lesson's actual content — not an illustration bolted onto a paragraph that already explained the point. A conversation must resolve something specific: a candidate's real concern gets surfaced and handled, a mistake gets shown and corrected, a moment of hesitation gets read correctly. A conversation that doesn't demonstrate a clear before-and-after is not doing its job and should be rewritten or cut.

## Roleplay Standards

Every lesson includes a roleplay ladder of exactly three difficulties — Foundational, Intermediate, and Expert (or the module's equivalent naming) — built around the same underlying skill the lesson's main conversation demonstrated. Difficulty must be a genuine, felt escalation: an easy roleplay gives the learner a cooperative, low-friction version of the situation; a difficult one gives them real resistance, a recognizable emotional stake, or a candidate actively testing the learner's honesty. Each roleplay names a specific persona, a specific objective, and a specific recommended answer with the reasoning behind it — never a generic "how would you respond" with no worked answer to compare against.

## Practice Standards

Every lesson includes practice exercises — short, written-response prompts distinct from the roleplay ladder — each paired with a model answer. A practice prompt should require the learner to actually produce a line in their own words, not just recognize a correct multiple-choice option. Practice exists to engage recall differently than reading does; a lesson that only offers roleplay and never asks the learner to write something themselves is incomplete.

## Conversation Quality Standards

A conversation must never read like two people taking turns delivering complete, polished paragraphs at each other. Real conversation has uneven turn lengths, short reactive replies, a line that trails off, a candidate who answers a different question than the one asked, and pace that changes with emotional weight. A conversation that reads smoothly from top to bottom on a first pass has probably not been tested against how an actual call sounds.

## Storytelling Standards

The Academy's five recurring personas — Rahul, Priya, Neha, Akash, and Emily as candidates, and Aayush as the Sales Executive — are not disposable examples; they are continuing characters whose situations should be remembered and honored across modules. A candidate's stated visa status, financial situation, family context, and prior objections in one module should still be true and referenced accurately if that same candidate reappears in a later module. Continuity is what makes the Academy feel like one story rather than fifty unrelated vignettes, and it is treated as a real content constraint, not a nice-to-have.

## Indian English Standards

SurelyPlaced's candidates and advisors are drawn from a population where Indian English is a natural, common register, and lesson conversations should reflect that authentically where it fits the persona and situation — particles like "only," "na," "no?" used the way they naturally occur, not as caricature or overused decoration. The bar is authenticity: a line should sound like something a real person in this specific context would actually say, never like a stereotype inserted to signal diversity. Where a persona or scene doesn't call for it, it should not be forced in.

## Cold Call Standards

A cold call lesson must open with the candidate not expecting the call, having no prior context about SurelyPlaced, and reacting the way a real person interrupted mid-day actually reacts — mild suspicion, distraction, a request to identify yourself, or an immediate "I'm busy." The Sales Executive's job in this standard is to earn ten more seconds honestly (naming that it's a cold call, stating a specific reason for calling, never disguising the call's nature) rather than trying to sound like the candidate already knows and expects them.

## LinkedIn Standards

A LinkedIn-sourced conversation should reflect how that channel actually works — the candidate has some minimal visibility into who's reaching out (a profile, a mutual signal) but still no real knowledge of SurelyPlaced specifically, and the opening should reference the actual channel honestly ("I found your profile," not a vague, unexplained "I got your information") rather than skip past how the contact happened.

## WhatsApp Standards

A WhatsApp-channel conversation should read differently in rhythm than a phone call — shorter messages, more natural gaps between them, a more informal register — and should be rendered using the Conversation Viewer's WhatsApp appearance mode so the format itself signals the channel before a learner reads a single line.

## Call Opening Standards

Every module's first real conversation the learner encounters should model a genuine, channel-appropriate opening — never skip straight into the substance of a call as if rapport and permission were already established. An opening earns the right to continue the conversation; it never assumes that right by default, and it never assumes the candidate already knows what SurelyPlaced is.

## No AI-Sounding Writing

Lesson prose and conversation dialogue must never contain generic AI-assistant phrasing — "in today's fast-paced environment," "it's important to note," "in conclusion," "navigate the complexities of," "delve into," or similarly generic filler. A sentence that could be dropped unchanged into an article about any unrelated topic does not belong in a Sales Academy lesson. Every sentence should read as if a specific, experienced Sales Trainer wrote it about this specific situation.

## No Large Paragraphs

Lesson prose outside the conversation widgets themselves is kept short — a paragraph is one to three sentences, never a dense block. A learner reading between calls, under time pressure, should never hit a wall of text. Where an idea needs more room, it is broken into its own short paragraph or moved into a widget (Coaching Notes, Common Mistakes) rather than left as one long paragraph trying to do too much.

## Maximum Reading Fatigue Rules

A single lesson should be readable start to finish in the time stated in its Estimated Time widget, honestly. If a lesson's actual content takes meaningfully longer to read than its stated estimate, the lesson is too long and should be split or trimmed — never silently left to run long while the estimate stays optimistic. Visual variety (conversation, then a decision point, then coaching notes, then mistakes, then roleplay) exists specifically to prevent the fatigue a purely long-form article would produce over the same length.

## Bullet Rules

Bullets are used for genuinely parallel, scannable items — a Lesson Objective's list of outcomes, a Quick Reference Panel's cheat-sheet items — never as a substitute for real sentences explaining a concept. A bulleted list inside prose that's actually trying to build an argument or tell a story is a sign the content should be rewritten as connected sentences instead.

## Example Rules

An illustrative example — a conversation, a persona, a scenario — is always clearly real within the Academy's own continuity (one of the five recurring personas, in a situation consistent with what's already been established about them) rather than a disposable one-off invented for a single lesson. Where a genuinely new, minor example is needed, it should still read as something that could plausibly be one of these five people, not an unrelated stranger introduced once and never mentioned again.

## Coaching Notes Rules

A Coaching Notes block explains why something worked, specifically — never a generic restatement of the lesson's title. "Why This Worked" must reference the actual line or moment from the conversation just shown, not a general principle that could apply to any lesson in the Academy. A Coaching Notes block that could be copy-pasted into a different lesson unchanged has failed at its one job.

## Widget Usage Philosophy

Every widget in the Academy's set — Conversation Viewer, Roleplay Card, Decision Point, Coaching Notes, Conversation Timeline, Quick Reference Panel, Mistakes Panel, Practice Box, Estimated Time, Module Completion, Manager Tip, Decision Tree — exists to support the conversation-first teaching method, never to decorate a page. A widget is used because it makes a real teaching moment clearer or more practice-able than plain prose would; it is never added because a lesson "should probably have one of those." Manager Tip is reserved for a genuinely critical, easy-to-miss coaching point, not a repeat of what Coaching Notes already said. Decision Tree is used sparingly — at most once per module, in a lesson specifically teaching a framework or decision structure — never as a default way to present a choice that a Decision Point widget would handle better.

## When Not to Use Widgets

Not every idea needs a widget. A short transitional sentence connecting two sections, a one-line bridge to the next module, or a simple clarifying aside belongs in plain prose, not forced into a Manager Tip or Coaching Notes block just to add visual variety. Manager scoring widgets (Call Scorecard) are not used in any new Sales Academy lesson — graded, manager-facing review is a separate platform capability, never something embedded inside content a learner is reading and practicing. New widgets are never invented to solve a one-off formatting need; the existing set is deliberately fixed, and a genuine gap in it is a product decision for `02_SALES_ACADEMY_PRODUCT_BLUEPRINT.md` to address, not something a content pass improvises around.

## Conversation Continuity Rules

A conversation that references an earlier call ("last time you mentioned...") must be consistent with what that earlier lesson actually established — the same stated concern, the same visa detail, the same emotional beat. Continuity errors erode the same trust the Academy is trying to teach advisors to build; a contradicted detail is a real defect, not a minor inconsistency, and should be caught the same way a broken internal link would be.

## Character Continuity Rules

Each of the five recurring personas has a consistent through-line that should deepen across modules rather than reset: Rahul's F1 OPT and STEM extension timeline, Priya's H1B analytical skepticism, Neha's H4 EAD and family decision-making, Akash's layoff and financial strain arc, Emily's straightforward US citizen case handled with the same care as every complex one. A future writer introducing a new recurring persona should give them the same depth and let their story develop across at least two modules before treating them as a fixed part of the roster.

## Learning Progression

Progression happens at three nested levels, and a lesson should never be written in isolation from any of them: within a lesson (a roleplay ladder moving from easy to difficult), within a module (five to ten lessons building toward that module's checklist lesson), and across the Academy (seven modules building toward the Complete Sales Call Walkthrough, which is designed to reference every earlier module by name as it happens). A lesson written without awareness of where it sits in all three levels is at real risk of either repeating material a learner has already mastered or assuming mastery of something not yet taught.
